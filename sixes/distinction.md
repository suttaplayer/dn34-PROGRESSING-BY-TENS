In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhamma qualities, categorized by number, leading to unbinding and the end of suffering and stress. Among these, **six dhammas are highlighted as being "on the side of distinction," specifically "six types of respect"**. This statement, initially given in brief, refers to a monk's cultivation of profound respect and deference for the Teacher, the Dhamma, the Saṅgha, the Training, heedfulness, and welcoming manners.

**Situation:**
The core situation presented is the emphasis on **specific qualities that lead to distinction or progress in the holy life**. These are not merely passive attitudes but active engagements that shape a monk's conduct and mind. A monk who cultivates these respects is recognized as someone whose path is characterized by advancement and steadfastness, rather than decline. This framework suggests that genuine spiritual development is intrinsically linked to one's reverence for the fundamental pillars of the Buddhist path and the community that upholds it.

**Complication:**
The sources implicitly and explicitly describe the challenges and negative consequences when these six types of respect are *not* upheld. Lack of respect for the Teacher, Dhamma, Saṅgha, training, heedfulness, and welcoming manners can lead to **decline and confusion of the True Dhamma**. For instance, if monks learn poorly memorized suttas with ill-placed words and phrases, the meaning can be wrongly inferred, leading to the confusion and disappearance of the True Dhamma. Similarly, being hard to correct, lacking endurance, and not taking instruction respectfully also contribute to this decline. The Dhamma itself is understood to be for those who are **modest, content, reclusive, persistent, mindful, concentrated, discerning, and enjoy non-objectification**, not for those who are self-aggrandizing, discontent, entangled, lazy, confused in mindfulness, unconcentrated, or weak in discernment. When individuals are steeped in such negative traits, they tend to **disparage and dispute**, creating disharmony within the community. A monk who dwells without respect or deference for the Teacher, Dhamma, Saṅgha, or training is operating on the "side of decline". Furthermore, if one does not act attentively or suitably, they are **incapable of attaining what is to be witnessed**.

**Resolution:**
Cultivating these six types of respect is crucial for a monk's **stability, non-confusion, and non-disappearance of the True Dhamma**.
1.  **Respect for the Teacher**: This means holding **conviction in the Tathāgata's awakening** and acknowledging him as the "worthy & rightly self-awakened, consummate in clear-knowing & conduct, well-gone, an expert with regard to the cosmos, unexcelled trainer of people fit to be tamed, teacher of devas & human beings, awakened, blessed". A disciple with conviction in the Teacher's message understands that the Teacher knows and they are merely a disciple. The Teacher's Dhamma is taught "through direct knowledge, not without direct knowledge; with a cause, not without a cause; with marvels, not without marvels".
2.  **Respect for the Dhamma**: This involves recognizing the Dhamma as "well taught by the Blessed One, to be seen here & now, timeless, inviting verification, pertinent, to be experienced by the observant for themselves". A monk who makes the Dhamma their dwelling and delight, pondering it and calling it to mind, **does not fall away from the true Dhamma**. The Dhamma, being "connected with the goal... fundamental to the holy life... leads to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, calming, direct knowledge, self-awakening, unbinding". Well-memorized Dhamma, with phrases well-placed, leads to **right inference of meaning, contributing to the Dhamma's stability**.
3.  **Respect for the Saṅgha**: The Saṅgha comprises the Blessed One's disciples who have **"practiced well… straight-forwardly… methodically… masterfully"**. They are "deserving of gifts, deserving of hospitality, deserving of offerings, deserving of respect, the incomparable field of merit for the world". Finding respect for one's fellows in the holy life allows one to **grow in the True Dhamma, "like an excellent seed in a field," and stand "right in the presence of unbinding"**. Living with the Saṅgha with proper respect leads to ease and mutual support.
4.  **Respect for the Training**: The training involves being "virtuous," dwelling "restrained in accordance with the Pāṭimokkha, consummate in his behavior & sphere of activity," and training "seeing danger in the slightest faults". This training encompasses **unexcelled virtue, concentration, discernment, and release**. A person "consummate in view" finds it impossible to live without respect or deference for this training.
5.  **Respect for Heedfulness**: Heedfulness is an exhortation to "be heedful, monks, mindful, virtuous. With your resolves well-concentrated, look after your minds". It is a quality that is **"very helpful"** and leads to the **ending of stress**. Developing heedfulness, especially through mindfulness, allows for "distinction" and **prevents the True Dhamma from fading away after the Tathāgata's unbinding**.
6.  **Respect for Welcoming Manners**: This translates to cultivating **"bodily acts of goodwill"** towards fellow practitioners, leading to "amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity". This fosters an environment where the community can dwell **"harmoniously, cordially, without dispute, becoming like milk mixed with water, regarding one another with affectionate eyes"**.

By upholding these six types of respect, a monk ensures not only their own spiritual progress towards gnosis and non-return, but also contributes to the stability, clarity, and enduring presence of the True Dhamma for the benefit and happiness of all beings.
