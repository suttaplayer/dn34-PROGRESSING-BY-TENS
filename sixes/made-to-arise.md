In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta presents a framework of Dhammas, including the "Six persevering dwellings" as qualities that should be made to arise. This teaching focuses on a monk's interaction with sensory input and the cultivation of a specific mental state.

### Situation

Human experience is often characterized by an immediate and often intense reaction to sensory stimuli. When encountering agreeable forms, sounds, aromas, flavors, tactile sensations, or ideas, individuals tend to become gladdened and infatuated. This relishing, welcoming, and fastening to agreeable sensory input causes delight to arise, leading to impassionment and becoming fettered. This "desire-passion with regard to the eye" and other sense bases is identified as a **defilement of the mind**.

### Complication

This habitual lack of restraint over the sense faculties leads to significant suffering. When a monk "dwells without restraint over the faculty of the eye, the mind is stained with forms cognizable via the eye". This staining results in a lack of joy, rapture, and calm, causing the individual to dwell in pain with an uncentered mind, ultimately being "reckoned simply as one who dwells in heedlessness". The sources further indicate that unskillful thoughts, particularly those "connected with desire, aversion, or delusion," are detrimental, as they "produce blindness, produce lack of vision, produce lack of knowledge, lead to the cessation of discernment, side with vexation, and are not conducive to Unbinding". Furthermore, if one relishes, welcomes, or remains fastened to even positive states like equanimity, this creates clinging or sustenance, preventing the mind from becoming "totally unbound". The "six persevering dwellings" are noted as "hard to penetrate" dhammas, highlighting the inherent challenge in mastering these practices.

### Resolution

The path to resolving this suffering involves cultivating the "Six persevering dwellings". This practice requires a monk, upon encountering any sensory input—forms via the eye, sounds via the ear, aromas via the nose, flavors via the tongue, tactile sensations via the body, or ideas via the intellect—to remain **equanimous, mindful, & alert**, neither gladdened nor saddened. This consistent practice of **guarding the doors of one's senses** ensures that one "does not grasp at any theme or details by which—if he were to dwell without restraint over the faculty of the eye—evil, unskillful qualities such as greed or distress might assail him". By maintaining this **noble restraint**, one becomes "inwardly sensitive to the pleasure of being blameless".

This leads to a profound shift: "the arisen agreeable… disagreeable… agreeable & disagreeable (reaction) ceases, and **equanimity takes a stance**". As the "defilements of awareness are abandoned," the mind "is inclined to renunciation" and "feels malleable for the direct knowing of those qualities worth realizing". When the monk attends to the themes of concentration, uplifted energy, and equanimity, his mind becomes "pliant, malleable, luminous, & not brittle," and "rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents". This practice also contributes to developing **mindfulness as a factor for awakening**, which is "dependent on seclusion, dependent on dispassion, dependent on cessation, resulting in letting go".

Ultimately, through this practice, a monk "has cut through craving, has ripped off the fetter, and—from rightly breaking through conceit—has put an end to suffering & stress". Such a person is described as "utterly complete, utterly free from bonds, a follower of the utterly holy life, utterly consummate". The state of being "unagitated" through "lack of clinging or sustenance" means that "there is no coming into being of the origination of future birth, aging, death, or stress". This path, among the best of paths, serves to "purify vision" and "put an end to suffering & stress". By developing these qualities, a monk is capable of realizing "the unexcelled cooled state" and attaining "arahantship". It is through such practices, like developing goodwill as an awareness-release, that one finds "the escape from ill will". The culmination is a state where "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world".
