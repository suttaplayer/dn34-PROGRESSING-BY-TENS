In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta outlines a comprehensive framework of Dhamma qualities that progress in categories of ten. Within this framework, **"Eight forms of wrongness: wrong view, wrong resolve, wrong speech, wrong action, wrong livelihood, wrong effort, wrong mindfulness, wrong concentration"** are specifically identified as dhammas that "should be abandoned". This statement, though brief, points to a core aspect of the Buddha's teaching on what hinders spiritual progress and how to overcome it.

Here's a detailed analysis following the "Situation, Complication, Resolution" (SCR) format:

### Situation

The situation is the presence of **"eight forms of wrongness"** that are detrimental to one's spiritual path and lead to undesirable outcomes. These are: wrong view, wrong resolve, wrong speech, wrong action, wrong livelihood, wrong effort, wrong mindfulness, and wrong concentration. These wrong qualities are fundamentally "unskillful", manifesting as misconduct in body, speech, and mind. They are rooted in **greed, aversion, and delusion**, which are described as "inside stains, inside enemies, inside foes, inside murderers, inside adversaries" and the primary "roots of what is unskillful".

**Wrong view**, as the forerunner of these wrongnesses, is characterized by beliefs such as "There is nothing given, nothing offered, nothing sacrificed" or that there is "no fruit or result of good or bad actions". It encompasses views that are "without cause & without condition" or solely "caused by what was done in the past," which negate the importance of desire and effort in one's present actions. When a person holds wrong view, it leads to a cascade of other wrong qualities, including wrong resolve, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration, ultimately resulting in failure.

Specific forms of misconduct include **false speech**, such as lying under questioning, **divisive speech**, intended to break people apart, **harsh speech**, and **idle chatter** that is not factual or purposeful. **Wrong bodily actions** involve taking life, taking what is not given (stealing), and engaging in sexual misconduct. These actions, rooted in wrong view, are said to lead a person, upon the body's breakup after death, to "a plane of deprivation, a bad destination, a lower realm, hell".

### Complication

The persistence of these "eight forms of wrongness" arises from deeper psychological and cognitive issues. At the heart of the problem is **ignorance**, which "precedes the arrival of unskillful qualities" and is identified as a "great delusion" causing continued wandering through cycles of birth and death.

The sources highlight how **inappropriate attention** (ayoniso manasikāra) is a significant factor in the arising and increase of unskillful qualities. If a monk attends inappropriately to ideas, "unarisen effluents arise, and arisen effluents increase". Conversely, inappropriate attention is also listed as a single "dhamma... on the side of decline".

Furthermore, **clinging** (upādāna), defined as "whatever desire & passion there is with regard to the five clinging-aggregates," is a requisite condition for suffering, leading to renewed "becoming, birth, aging, illness, & death, sorrow, lamentation pain, distress, & despair". This deep-seated attachment reinforces the cycle of wrong actions and their undesirable results.

Other factors complicating the abandonment of these wrongnesses include the **five hindrances** (sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, uncertainty), which are seen as "food for ignorance" and "mental blockages" that prevent the mind from tending towards "ardency, commitment, perseverance, or exertion". Even something like **stinginess** regarding the Dhamma itself can hinder progress towards higher states of consciousness and enlightenment.

### Resolution

The resolution to abandon these "eight forms of wrongness" lies in the **development of the noble eightfold path**, which is presented as the very opposite and antidote to wrongness. This path is the "middle way" that leads to "stilling, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to unbinding".

The path begins with **right view**, which is considered the "forerunner". It involves accurately discerning what is skillful and unskillful, along with their roots, which are **lack of greed, lack of aversion, and lack of delusion**. Right view also entails understanding the dependent co-arising of phenomena like nutriment, stress, aging and death, birth, becoming, clinging, craving, feeling, contact, the six sense media, name-and-form, consciousness, fabrications, and ignorance, and realizing that their cessation is the path to liberation. This proper understanding, when "supported by virtue, supported by learning, supported by discussion, supported by tranquility, [and] supported by insight," has "awareness-release as its fruit" and "discernment-release as its fruit".

The practice of **right speech** involves abandoning lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter. Instead, one practices speaking truthfully, in unity, gently, and purposefully, offering words "worth treasuring, timely, reasonable, circumscribed, connected with the goal". **Right action** similarly means abstaining from taking life, stealing, and sexual misconduct.

**Right effort** entails actively making an "effort for the attaining of the as-yet-unattained, the reaching of the as-yet-unreached, the realization of the as-yet-unrealized". **Right mindfulness** is cultivated by maintaining a focused awareness on the body, feelings, mind, and mental qualities, being "ardent, alert, & mindful," and "subduing greed & distress". This "mindfulness immersed in the body" is considered "very helpful" for purity and the realization of unbinding. Furthermore, a monk should be "always mindful" in all postures and actions.

Finally, **right concentration** is developed through the practice of jhāna, a state of deep mental seclusion from sensuality and unskillful qualities, leading to rapture and pleasure, and eventually to "purity of equanimity & mindfulness, neither pleasure nor pain". This can be fostered through "immeasurable concentration" based on qualities like goodwill, compassion, empathetic joy, or equanimity. Such "noble right concentration" is characterized by "singleness of mind equipped with these seven factors" of the path.

Ultimately, the complete abandonment of these eight forms of wrongness through the noble eightfold path leads to the cessation of suffering and stress, culminating in "unbinding" (Nibbāna).
