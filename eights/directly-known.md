In the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhamma categories, among which are "eight dhammas that should be directly known: Eight dimensions of (mental) mastery". This statement highlights a profound aspect of spiritual development, focusing on the progressive cultivation and understanding of various states of consciousness.

Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of this statement, presented in the Situation, Complication, and Resolution (SCR) format:

**Situation**
The "eight dimensions of (mental) mastery" are identified as crucial *dhammas* (teachings or qualities) that a practitioner should "directly know". These dimensions represent a progressive path of cultivating and gaining control over one's perceptions and states of awareness, moving beyond ordinary sensory experience into increasingly refined and subtle mental realms. This cultivation is part of the "unexcelled development of the faculties in the discipline of the noble one".

**Complication**
The complexity and subtlety of these dimensions pose a significant challenge to their direct knowing and mastery. The path requires moving from the most basic perceptions of physical forms to abstract, formless states, and ultimately to the cessation of all perception and feeling. The "eight dimensions of (mental) mastery" are described as follows:
*   **First dimension**: When one has a single perception of form internally and **sees forms externally as limited, beautiful & ugly**. By mastering them, one gains the perception, 'I know; I see'.
*   **Second dimension**: When one has a single perception of form internally and **sees forms externally as immeasurable, beautiful & ugly**. By mastering them, one gains the perception, 'I know; I see'.
*   **Third dimension**: One **is intent only on the beautiful**.
*   **Fourth dimension**: With the complete transcending of perceptions of physical form, the disappearance of perceptions of resistance, and not heeding perceptions of multiplicity, one perceives 'Infinite space' and **enters and remains in the dimension of the infinitude of space**.
*   **Fifth dimension**: With the complete transcending of the dimension of the infinitude of space, one perceives 'Infinite consciousness' and **enters and remains in the dimension of the infinitude of consciousness**.
*   **Sixth dimension**: With the complete transcending of the dimension of the infinitude of consciousness, one perceives 'There is nothing' and **enters and remains in the dimension of nothingness**.
*   **Seventh dimension**: With the complete transcending of the dimension of nothingness, one **enters and remains in the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception**.
*   **Eighth dimension**: With the complete transcending of the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception, one **enters & remains in the cessation of perception and feeling**.

The Dhamma related to these profound insights is "not easily realized by those overcome with aversion & passion," being "abstruse, subtle, deep, hard to see, going against the flow". It requires the practitioner's consciousness to be "neither externally scattered & diffused, nor internally positioned". These are "hard to penetrate" dhammas, requiring deep cultivation.

**Resolution**
The purpose of directly knowing and mastering these eight dimensions is to achieve ultimate liberation from suffering and stress. When one's consciousness is "neither externally scattered & diffused, nor internally positioned" and free "from lack of clinging or sustenance," there arises "no seed for the conditions of future birth, aging, death, or stress". This state of "non-agitation is caused by lack of clinging or sustenance".

Through such mastery, the practitioner's mind becomes "pliant, malleable, luminous, & not brittle," and "rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents". The ultimate outcome of this development is the "effluent-free awareness-release & discernment-release," which is directly known and realized "right in the here & now". This culminates in the profound realization that "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world". The Dhamma, including these "eighty dhammas," is affirmed as "true, genuine, real, not unreal, not otherwise," having been "rightly awakened to by the Tathāgata".
