In the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Venerable Sāriputta presents a framework of Dhammas, including "Eight grounds for the arousal of energy". These eight grounds are considered to be "on the side of distinction", meaning they lead to positive spiritual development. This teaching outlines specific circumstances that should motivate a monk to apply persistent effort toward liberation and the ultimate goal of the holy life.

**Situation:**
The "Eight grounds for the arousal of energy" refer to various real-life situations that a monk might encounter, such as undertaking or completing physical tasks, embarking on or concluding journeys, experiencing illness, or having varying levels of access to sustenance. These are universal experiences that, when viewed with discernment, become opportunities for spiritual growth rather than distractions or impediments.

**Complication:**
The inherent "complication" in each of these situations is the recognition that they can potentially hinder one's deep spiritual engagement or, conversely, that they present a fleeting opportunity that should be seized. For instance, a monk might anticipate that when "doing this work, it will not be easy to attend to the Buddha's message". Similarly, an illness might provoke the thought that "my life will come to an end", or even recovering from an illness might lead to the concern that "this illness might recur". Even seemingly favorable conditions, like having "much food, much alms-food", carry a subtle complication if the ease they provide is not channeled into spiritual endeavor.

**Resolution:**
The "resolution" in each instance is the monk's deliberate and proactive decision to harness the particular circumstance as an impetus for intensive spiritual practice. The consistent aim, regardless of the specific situation, is to **"make an effort beforehand"** or **"make an effort now"** for the **"attaining of the as-yet-unattained, the reaching of the as-yet-unreached, the realization of the as-yet-unrealized"**. This demonstrates a constant mindfulness and commitment to the path to liberation.

Here is a detailed analysis of each of the eight grounds for the arousal of energy:

*   **1. Impending Work:** If a monk "has some work to do" and anticipates that "when I am doing this work, it will not be easy to attend to the Buddha's message", he resolves to make an effort beforehand to achieve the unreached spiritual goal.
*   **2. Work Completed:** Having finished a task, a monk reflects that it "was not easy to attend to the Buddha's message" during the work. This realization prompts him to "make an effort now" for spiritual advancement.
*   **3. Impending Journey:** When a monk "has to go on a journey", he foresees that it "will not be easy to attend to the Buddha's message" during travel, motivating him to strive beforehand.
*   **4. Journey Completed:** After completing a journey, a monk recognizes that spiritual focus "was not easy" during that period, leading him to "make an effort now" for deeper realization.
*   **5. Illness Arising:** If a monk "is not very well" and "some illness has arisen in him", leading to the thought that "my life will come to an end", he commits to an immediate effort for the unreached.
*   **6. Recovery from Illness:** Having "recovered from his illness", a monk reflects that "this illness might recur", prompting him to make an effort while healthy.
*   **7. Little Sustenance:** With "little food, little alms-food", and understanding that "it will not be easy for me to be doing my body's business", a monk utilizes this scarcity as a catalyst to strive for liberation.
*   **8. Much Sustenance:** Even when a monk "has much food, much alms-food" and perceives that "it will be easy for me to be doing my body's business", he makes an effort to use these favorable conditions optimally for the attainment of the unreached.
