The sources indicate that the Blessed One and his enlightened disciples taught various Dhamma categories for the realization of unbinding and the cessation of suffering and stress. Among these teachings, Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhamma qualities in the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), which includes a category of "eight emancipations" that are to be realized.

Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of "Which eight dhammas should be realized? Eight emancipations":

**Situation:**
The "eight emancipations" are a crucial set of **Dhammas to be realized** for attaining **unbinding** and putting an end to suffering and stress. These are specific stages of mental liberation that describe a progression toward profound peace.

**Complication:**
The journey to these emancipations necessitates overcoming deeply ingrained patterns of perception and attachment. An **uninstructed run-of-the-mill person** often perceives the world in terms of "I-making" or "mine-making conceit-obsession" concerning their conscious body and external phenomena. Such individuals are prone to suffering because they have not **comprehended** the true nature of things, often supposing things about earth or other phenomena as "mine" due to a lack of understanding. They may prioritize "bombast" over genuine "cross-questioning" and **dissecting** the Dhamma, hindering their path to deep meaning. Furthermore, unskillful thoughts, particularly those "imbued with sensuality, ill-will, [and] harmfulness," can **produce blindness, lack of vision, and lack of knowledge**, thereby impeding the cessation of discernment and preventing unbinding. The challenge lies in transcending these ordinary ways of perceiving and reacting to the world to achieve genuine release. Even for those practicing, qualities like "laziness" or "restlessness" can keep the mind from being "rightly concentrated" for the ending of effluents.

**Resolution:**
The "eight emancipations" guide a monk through a sequential process of mental release, where the mind becomes **pliant, malleable, luminous, & not brittle**, and "rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents". This leads to **awareness-release** and **discernment-release**, culminating in the **total ending of effluents** and **unbinding**.

The eight emancipations to be realized are:
*   **1. Possessed of form, one sees forms**: This initial emancipation describes the ability to perceive physical forms while still internally identifying with form. It marks a foundational level of awareness within the realm of physical existence.
*   **2. Not percipient of form internally, one sees forms externally**: Progressing from the first, this stage involves transcending the internal perception of one's own form, allowing for the observation of external forms without attachment or self-identification with one's own body.
*   **3. One is intent only on the beautiful**: At this stage, the meditator directs their focus towards attractive or pleasing forms, often as a preliminary step in developing deeper states of concentration, such as the initial stages of jhāna where rapture and pleasure arise from seclusion.
*   **4. With the complete transcending of perceptions of (physical) form, with the disappearance of perceptions of resistance, and not attending to perceptions of multiplicity, (perceiving,) 'Infinite space,' one enters & remains in the dimension of the infinitude of space**: This marks a significant shift to formless attainments. It involves relinquishing all perceptions connected to physical form and any sense of resistance, allowing the mind to expand into a boundless and infinite awareness of space. This is described as a "peaceful liberation that transcends form".
*   **5. With the complete transcending of the dimension of the infinitude of space, (perceiving,) 'Infinite consciousness,' one enters & remains in the dimension of the infinitude of consciousness**: Building upon the previous stage, the practitioner transcends the perception of infinite space to directly experience and dwell in the boundless nature of consciousness itself.
*   **6. With the complete transcending of the dimension of the infinitude of consciousness, (perceiving,) 'There is nothingness,' one enters & remains in the dimension of nothingness**: This emancipation involves moving beyond the perception of infinite consciousness to a state where the mind apprehends nothing at all, a realm of utter emptiness.
*   **7. With the complete transcending of the dimension of nothingness, one enters & remains in the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception**: This is a highly refined and subtle state of consciousness where neither full perception nor the complete absence of perception is present. It is the penultimate stage before ultimate liberation.
*   **8. With the complete transcending of the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception, one enters & remains in the cessation of perception and feeling**: This is the ultimate and most profound emancipation, representing the **complete cessation of all perceptions and feelings**. This state leads to the total end of suffering and stress and the full realization of **unbinding**. It is considered the "fruit of gnosis [arahantship]" and a state where there is "nothing felt" but is still experienced as pleasure. Upon achieving this, one's mind is **unagitated from lack of clinging or sustenance**, and there is "no seed for the conditions of future birth, aging, death, or stress". Such a person has "cut through craving, has ripped off the fetter," and **put an end to suffering & stress**. They are said to be **"released both ways"**, having accomplished their task with **"nothing further for this world"**. This complete release is attained by one who "knows & sees" the cessation of effluents.
