In the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhamma principles, categorized by number, leading to unbinding and the ending of suffering and stress. Among these are the "Eight thoughts of a great person," which are qualities that should be developed. These thoughts describe the ideal disposition and practice of one who seeks to live the holy life in line with the Dhamma.

### Situation

The core situation is the aspiration for a path that leads to the cessation of suffering and stress. The Dhamma is taught for this purpose, leading to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, stilling, direct knowledge, self-awakening, and unbinding. Living heedful, ardent, and resolute is fundamental to this path. The Dhamma itself is described as admirable in its beginning, middle, and end, expounding the holy life as entirely perfect and surpassingly pure.

### Complication

The "Eight thoughts of a great person" address various unskillful mental qualities that can hinder a monk's progress, serving as complications to the holy life:

*   **Self-aggrandizement:** This Dhamma is for one who is modest, not for one who is self-aggrandizing. A modest monk **does not want it to be known that 'He is modest'**.
*   **Discontent:** This Dhamma is for one who is content, not for one who is discontent. Discontent arises from not being satisfied with requisites.
*   **Entanglement:** This Dhamma is for one who is reclusive, not for one who is entangled. Entanglement arises from excessive interaction with others, especially when it distracts from seclusion.
*   **Laziness:** This Dhamma is for one whose persistence is aroused, not for one who is lazy. Laziness obstructs the effort needed for spiritual attainment.
*   **Confused Mindfulness:** This Dhamma is for one whose mindfulness is established, not for one whose mindfulness is confused. Without established mindfulness, one cannot remember and recall things accurately.
*   **Unconcentrated Mind:** This Dhamma is for one whose mind is concentrated, not for one whose mind is unconcentrated. An unconcentrated mind is unable to enter into the deeper states of meditation (jhānas).
*   **Weak Discernment:** This Dhamma is for one endowed with discernment, not for one whose discernment is weak. Weak discernment prevents one from understanding the arising and passing away of phenomena, which is crucial for ending stress.
*   **Delight in Objectification:** This Dhamma is for one who enjoys non-objectification, who delights in non-objectification, not for one who enjoys and delights in objectification. Objectification involves clinging to concepts and perceptions, which perpetuates suffering.

### Resolution

The resolution involves cultivating the positive qualities outlined in the eight thoughts, which lead to profound states of mind and ultimately to liberation:

*   **Modesty (not self-aggrandizing):** A monk cultivates modesty by **not wanting to be known for his modest qualities**. This reflects an absence of conceit, a quality that, when broken through, leads to the end of suffering and stress.
*   **Contentment (not discontent):** A monk is **content with any old robe cloth, almsfood, lodging, or medicinal requisites**. This contentment is a factor of the contemplative life, reducing vexation over material things.
*   **Reclusiveness (not entanglement):** A monk, with his mind set on seclusion and relishing renunciation, **converses with visitors only as much as necessary for them to take their leave**. This practice of internal emptiness and detachment helps in abandoning craving.
*   **Aroused Persistence (not laziness):** A monk ensures his **persistence is aroused and unsluggish**. This effort is crucial for attaining the as-yet-unattained, reaching the as-yet-unreached, and realizing the as-yet-unrealized.
*   **Established Mindfulness (not confused):** A monk becomes **mindful, endowed with excellent proficiency in mindfulness, remembering & able to call to mind even things that were done & said long ago**. Mindfulness is key to purifying beings and realizing unbinding.
*   **Concentrated Mind (not unconcentrated):** Through seclusion, a monk develops concentration by entering and remaining in the four jhānas, culminating in **purity of equanimity & mindfulness, neither pleasure nor pain**. A concentrated mind discerns in line with what has come into being, leading to the understanding of stress and its cessation.
*   **Discernment (not weak):** A monk becomes **discerning, endowed with discernment of arising & passing away—noble, penetrating, leading to the right ending of stress**. Discernment is the surpassing state for the ending of suffering and stress, and it nurtures the mind's release from effluents.
*   **Non-objectification (not delighting in objectification):** The monk's mind **leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, & released in the cessation of objectification**. This involves moving beyond perceptions and categories of objectification, leading to the end of obsessions, resistance, and views.

By cultivating these eight thoughts, a practitioner can achieve the four jhānas and eventually **enter & remain in the effluent-free awareness-release & discernment-release, having directly known & realized them for himself right in the here & now**. This signifies the cutting through of craving, the ripping off of fetters, and the end of suffering and stress. This practice leads to mental pliability, malleability, luminosity, and concentration for the ending of effluents. The Dhamma provides the path to this state, a path of peace and the stopping of birth.
