In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta outlines a framework of Dhammas, which includes nine specific dhammas rooted in craving that should be abandoned. This framework highlights how craving acts as a chain leading to suffering and unskillful actions.

Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of this statement, presented in a Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) format:

### Situation

The fundamental **root of suffering and entanglement in the world is craving**. The sources indicate that beings wander and transmigrate for a long time, fettered by craving, which is described as an "ensnarer that has flowed along, spread out, and caught hold, with which this world is smothered & enveloped". This craving is directly linked to the origination of stress.

Specifically, craving initiates a chain of dependencies:
*   **Seeking** is dependent on craving.
*   **Acquisition** is dependent on seeking.
*   **Ascertainment** is dependent on acquisition.
*   **Desire & passion** are dependent on ascertainment.

These initial manifestations of craving are problematic because any desire-passion for phenomena, whether related to the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or intellect, acts as a **defilement of the mind**. Sensual desire itself is identified as an "obstacle, a hindrance that overwhelms awareness and weakens discernment". The Blessed One has emphasized that "sensual pleasures are of little satisfaction, much stress, much despair, & greater drawbacks". Furthermore, the five clinging-aggregates (form, feeling, perception, fabrications, consciousness), which are the basis of experience, are explicitly rooted in desire or craving, and any passion or delight regarding them constitutes clinging.

### Complication

The progression from initial craving and desire-passion escalates into more deeply entrenched and overtly harmful states. From **desire & passion** arises **attachment**. This attachment signifies a deep entanglement with the world, where one adheres to "what's seen, heard, & sensed".

This attachment then leads to **possessiveness**, which in turn results in **stinginess**. Stinginess is identified as a significant detrimental quality, particularly "stinginess as to the Dhamma," and its abandonment is necessary for entering states of deep concentration (jhāna) or realizing noble fruits. The sources describe a range of "stains" and "flaws" that prevent proper contemplative practice, including possessiveness, ill will, anger, spitefulness, envy, and selfishness.

The final stage in this progression, stemming from stinginess, is **defensiveness**. Dependent on this defensiveness, "various evil, unskillful phenomena come into play: the taking up of sticks & knives; conflicts, quarrels, & disputes; accusations, divisive speech, & lies". This highlights how the internal cultivation of craving, attachment, and possessiveness ultimately erupts into overt unskillful bodily, verbal, and mental actions. These are the kinds of actions that make one "not practicing the practice proper to a contemplative".

### Resolution

The path to **alleviating suffering and achieving unbinding** lies in the **abandonment of these nine dhammas rooted in craving**. The fundamental purpose of the Dhamma is to lead to "utter disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to stilling, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to unbinding". The Dhamma taught by the Blessed One is for "the abandoning of passion, for the abandoning of aversion, for the abandoning of delusion", which are the fundamental fires of existence.

The process of abandonment involves:

*   **Cultivating disenchantment and dispassion:** One should continually cultivate "disenchantment with regard to form… feeling… perception… fabrications… consciousness". This disenchantment, when developed, leads to dispassion and ultimately to release. The fading of passion and ignorance directly leads to awareness-release and discernment-release.
*   **Ending craving and clinging:** The ending of craving directly leads to the "ending of action" and thus the "ending of stress". The ultimate goal of unbinding (nibbāna) is achieved through "the pacification of all fabrications; the relinquishing of all acquisitions; the ending of craving; dispassion; cessation; unbinding".
*   **Abandoning desire-passion:** All that is inconstant, stressful, and not-self (such as form, feeling, perception, fabrications, and consciousness) should be approached by abandoning desire-passion.
*   **Ceasing objectification:** When there is "nothing... to relish, welcome, or remain fastened to" with regard to the perceptions and categories of objectification, then "that is the end of the obsessions of passion... That is the end of taking up rods & bladed weapons, of arguments, quarrels, disputes, accusations, divisive speech, & false speech".
*   **Developing noble perceptions:** Cultivating "nine perceptions...of great fruit, of great benefit" which gain "a footing in the deathless" are crucial for this abandonment. These include the perceptions of unattractiveness, death, loathsomeness in food, distaste for every world, inconstancy, stress in inconstancy, not-self in stress, abandoning, and dispassion.
*   **Abandoning hindrances:** The abandonment of the five hindrances—sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty—is essential for developing concentration and discerning liberation.

By systematically abandoning these craving-rooted dhammas, a monk becomes capable of realizing arahantship, having abandoned passion, aversion, delusion, and other related defilements like anger, resentment, arrogance, insolence, envy, and stinginess. This practice leads to a state where "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world".
