Here is an analysis of the statement regarding the "nine dhammas rooted in appropriate attention," presented in the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), structured in the Situation, Complication, and Resolution (SCR) format.

### Situation

The starting point for this transformative process is **appropriate attention**. This quality is considered to be "on the side of distinction", signifying its positive and beneficial nature. When a disciple of the noble ones listens to the Dhamma with a receptive, attentive mind, "focusing his entire awareness, lending ear," their "five hindrances don't exist, and seven factors for awakening go to the completion of their development". This initial state of focused, appropriate attention lays the groundwork for the subsequent unfolding of skillful qualities.

### Complication

Following the cultivation of appropriate attention, a progressive chain of mental and physical states begins to unfold, each leading to the next, as described:

*   **Joy is Born:** "In one who is appropriately attentive, **joy is born**". This joy is specifically noted to arise when one is "sensitive to the meaning, sensitive to the Dhamma". It indicates that the mind is "not overcome with passion, not overcome with aversion, not overcome with delusion" and "heads straight" based on the Dhamma.
*   **Rapture is Born:** "When he is joyful, **rapture is born**". Rapture, born from joy, is a factor for awakening.
*   **Body Grows Calm:** "In one who is enraptured at heart, the **body grows calm**". This calm state of the body is a direct result of the rapture experienced.
*   **Pleasure is Felt:** "When the body is calm, one feels **pleasure**". Pleasure is identified as one of the three types of feelings, alongside pain and neither-pleasure-nor-pain.
*   **Mind Becomes Concentrated:** "Feeling pleasure, the **mind becomes concentrated**". This concentration is a factor of awakening and is crucial for deeper insight. When a monk "attends periodically to the theme of concentration, attends periodically to the theme of uplifted energy, attends periodically to the theme of equanimity," their "mind is pliant, malleable, luminous, & not brittle" and "rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents". Concentration, when "nurtured with virtue," leads to "great fruit, great reward".
*   **Knowing & Seeing What Has Come to Be:** "When the mind is concentrated, one **knows & sees what has come to be**". This discernment, cultivated through concentration, involves recognizing fundamental truths. A "concentrated monk discerns things as they have come to be," such as the inconstancy of the eye, forms, consciousness, contact, and the feelings arising from them. The process of "knowing & seeing" is explicitly linked to the "ending of effluents". This includes understanding the four noble truths: "'This is stress,'… 'This is the origination of stress,'… 'This is the cessation of stress,'… 'This is the path of practice leading to the cessation of stress'".
*   **Disenchantment:** "Knowing & seeing what has come to be, one is **disenchanted**". This disenchantment (nibbidā) arises from a clear understanding of reality, particularly the inconstancy, stress, and not-self nature of phenomena like form, feeling, perception, fabrications, and consciousness. It is one of the "perceptions for the maturing of release".
*   **Dispassion:** "Disenchanted, one becomes **dispassionate**". Dispassion (virāga) is a core aim of the Dhamma, leading to the "pacification of all fabrications, the relinquishing of all acquisitions, the ending of craving". It arises from the "fading of passion".

### Resolution

The culmination of this sequence, rooted in appropriate attention and progressing through joy, rapture, calm, pleasure, concentration, knowing and seeing, and disenchantment, is **release**.

*   **Release:** "From dispassion, one is **released**". This release is specifically termed "awareness-release" and "discernment-release", signifying a mind liberated from defilements. A monk who achieves this state "has cut through craving, has ripped off the fetter, and—from rightly breaking through conceit—has put an end to suffering & stress". The Dhamma is taught precisely for this "right ending of suffering & stress," which is "touched… through release". This ultimate freedom is described as "the unexcelled safety from bonds".
