**Situation**

The query centers on the specific statement from the *Dasuttara Sutta* (Dīgha Nikāya 34) that describes **nine factors of exertion for full purity**. This teaching is presented within a broader framework aimed at "attaining unbinding, for putting an end to suffering & stress, for releasing from all ties". The inherent situation is that of individuals seeking complete liberation and purity, implying a journey from a state of impurity and entanglement to one of ultimate freedom.

**Complication**

The path to full purity is multifaceted and challenging, requiring more than superficial adherence. The sources highlight that a mind "defiled by passion" cannot achieve release, and discernment does not develop when "defiled by ignorance". This points to internal hindrances that complicate the process of purification. Furthermore, the presence of impurities such as being unvirtuous, envious, or stingy actively prevents individuals from reaching positive destinations. Without a clear understanding of the specific exertions required for each aspect of purity—from ethical foundations to profound insight—practitioners risk stagnation or falling into wrong views and practices. True progress necessitates a diligent and active process of abandoning unskillful qualities and cultivating skillful ones.

**Resolution**

The nine factors of exertion for full purity provide a comprehensive and progressive framework for achieving liberation, integrating ethical conduct, mental discipline, and insightful wisdom:

1.  **Purity of Virtue (Sīla):** This is the **foundational element**, emphasizing blameless and upright conduct. A monk practices virtue by dwelling "restrained in accordance with the Pāṭimokkha, consummate in his behavior & sphere of activity," and recognizing "danger in the slightest faults". Such virtue serves as a "treasure" and cultivates a "sense of shame & compunction", leading to a pure bodily, verbal, and mental conduct.
2.  **Purity of Mind (Concentration/Samādhi):** This involves developing a **heightened state of concentration** to stabilize and unify the mind. Concentration, when "nurtured with virtue," yields "great fruit" and "great reward". Developing "immeasurable concentration"—such as that based on goodwill—leads to internal clarity, making the mind "pliant, malleable, luminous, & not brittle" for the ending of effluents.
3.  **Purity of View (Sammā-diṭṭhi):** This emphasizes the cultivation of **Right View**, which acts as the "forerunner" of the entire path. It involves discerning what is skillful and unskillful, their roots, and understanding the Four Noble Truths: stress, its origination, cessation, and the path leading to its cessation. A person with "verified confidence in the Dhamma" and consummate in right view is incapable of falling into self-identity views, doubt, or engaging in practices that lead to lower realms. Right view, supported by virtue, learning, discussion, tranquility, and insight, is directly linked to the attainment of awareness-release and discernment-release.
4.  **Purity of Overcoming Doubt:** This factor focuses on **eradicating uncertainty (vicikicchā)**, which is categorized as one of the "five lower fetters" and a hindrance. The arising of the "dustless, stainless Dhamma eye" is presented as a direct outcome that leads to the abandonment of doubt, instilling a deep and unshakable confidence in the teachings.
5.  **Purity of Knowledge & Vision of What Is & Is Not the Path:** This involves a **clear understanding of the correct spiritual path**, distinguishing it from false or incomplete doctrines. The Dhamma is consistently described as the path that leads to "disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, stilling, direct knowledge, self-awakening, and unbinding", making it essential to discern what truly leads to these outcomes.
6.  **Purity of Knowledge & Vision of the Way:** Building on the previous point, this emphasizes the **practical methods and steps of the path**, particularly the Noble Eightfold Path. It highlights the importance of sustained "persistence aroused" and "mindfulness established" to abandon unskillful qualities and develop skillful ones, thereby ensuring genuine progress toward liberation.
7.  **Purity of Knowledge & Vision:** This refers to the attainment of **direct knowledge (ñāṇa-dassana)**, a profound and immediate understanding of reality. This clear knowing is fundamental to the ending of effluents. The Buddha is revered for his "exceeding knowledge & vision" and for teaching the Dhamma through "direct knowledge, not without direct knowledge". This factor is crucial for progressing to release, as "first, Susima, there is the knowledge of the regularity of the Dhamma [dependent co-arising], after which there is the knowledge of unbinding".
8.  **Purity of Discernment (Paññā):** Discernment is the **wisdom** that allows one to penetrate the true nature of existence. It is central to the holy life, being described as its "surpassing state" and a "treasure". Discernment enables one to know "arising & passing away" and leads to the "right ending of stress". A mind "well shored-up by discernment" is ready for the ultimate affirmation: "'Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world'".
9.  **Purity of Release (Vimutti):** This is the **culmination of the practice**, signifying ultimate liberation from suffering. It manifests as "awareness-release & discernment-release" achieved through the complete "fading of passion" and "fading of ignorance". This final release is characterized by the total "ending of the effluents"—sensuality, becoming, and ignorance—and the "cessation of craving," resulting in a state of "unbinding" (nibbāna) where one is "free from hunger, totally unbound" and "there is no further becoming".

These nine factors together form a complete system of cultivation, leading the practitioner through progressive stages of purification until the ultimate goal of unbinding is realized.
