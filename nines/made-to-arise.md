Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of the statement regarding the nine perceptions that should be made to arise, presented in a "Situation, Complication, Resolution" format:

**Situation**

The Buddha teaches that nine specific perceptions should be cultivated and made to arise in one's awareness. These perceptions are: unattractiveness, death, loathsomeness in food, distaste for every world, inconstancy, stress in what is inconstant, not-self in what is stressful, abandoning, and dispassion. The cultivation and pursuit of these nine perceptions are stated to yield great fruit and benefit, securing a footing in the Deathless and ultimately leading to the Deathless itself. These teachings are based on fundamental properties of reality, such as the inconstancy, stressful nature, and non-self characteristic of all fabrications and phenomena, which remain true regardless of whether a Tathāgata appears in the world.

**Complication**

Without the development of these crucial perceptions, individuals remain entangled in suffering. An uninstructed person, for instance, does not discern fundamental truths like stress, its origination, cessation, or the path to its cessation, leading to continued suffering, aging, death, sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, and despairs. Beings tend to perceive things in terms of conventional "signs" and become attached to them, leading to the "bonds of death". The world, including its sensory experiences (eye, forms, ear, sounds, etc.), is described as being "aflame with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, the fire of delusion," and with "birth, aging & death, with sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, & despairs". This entanglement stems from a tendency to focus on the allure of "clingable phenomena," which develops craving and leads to the production of renewed suffering and stress. When the mind is overcome by self-identification views, it does not discern the escape from such views, and these views, being fixed and unsubdued, act as "lower fetters".

**Resolution**

The cultivation of these nine perceptions leads to liberation from suffering and the realization of unbinding. When a monk's awareness is steeped in the perception of unattractiveness or death, the mind naturally shrinks away from fervor for life, leading to equanimity or loathing. Similarly, frequently focusing on the inconstancy of things helps the mind detach from gains, offerings, and fame, fostering equanimity or loathing. By recognizing what is inconstant, stressful, and not-self, one is instructed to abandon desire-passion in relation to these phenomena. This practice helps to cultivate disenchantment with form, feeling, perception, fabrications, and consciousness, leading to their comprehension and ultimately to total release from sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair.

The ultimate goal is to eliminate "I-making" and "mine-making" conceit-obsessions, which, when absent, signify that a monk "has cut through craving, has ripped off the fetter, and—from rightly breaking through conceit—has put an end to suffering & stress". By letting go of clinging, particularly to perceptions and categories of objectification, one can end obsessions of passion, resistance, views, uncertainty, conceit, and ignorance, thereby bringing an end to various unskillful actions and disputes. This process culminates in the "cessation of objectification" and "stilling of objectification" through the fading and cessation of the six contact-media. This entire path begins with the "knowledge of the regularity of the Dhamma" (dependent co-arising), followed by the "knowledge of unbinding", and enables one to directly know and realize the Four Noble Truths—stress, its origination, its cessation, and the path leading to its cessation—thus leading to the discernment that "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world".
