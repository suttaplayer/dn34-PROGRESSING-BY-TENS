Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of the statement from the DASUTTARA SUTTA, presented in a Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) format:

### Situation

Venerable Sāriputta, in the DASUTTARA SUTTA, outlines a comprehensive framework of Dhamma qualities that progress by tens, categorizing them by their function, such as qualities that are helpful, should be developed, comprehended, or abandoned. Among these, he identifies **four specific knowledges that "should be made to arise"**: "knowledge with regard to the Dhamma, knowledge with regard to inference, knowledge with regard to encompassing (the minds of others), [and] knowledge of conventions". These are described as "true, genuine, real, not unreal, not otherwise" and "have been rightly awakened to by the Tathāgata".

### Complication

The brief mention of these four knowledges prompts a deeper inquiry into their specific meaning, how they are cultivated, and their role within the Buddhist path. They are not merely intellectual understandings but profound realizations integral to spiritual development.

1.  **Knowledge with regard to the Dhamma**: This refers to a deep and comprehensive understanding of the Buddha's teachings. It involves not just superficial learning but a thorough mastery and penetration of the Dhamma. Monks are expected to learn the Dhamma – including dialogues, narratives, explanations, verses, spontaneous exclamations, quotations, birth stories, amazing events, and question & answer sessions – and to listen to it often, retain it, discuss it, accumulate it, examine it with their minds, and well-penetrate it in terms of their views. This diligent study ensures that the meaning is "rightly inferred" from "well memorized" suttas, leading to the "stability, non-confusion, & non-disappearance of the True Dhamma". A disciple cultivates this knowledge with conviction, recognizing the Teacher's supremacy in knowing the Dhamma.

2.  **Knowledge with regard to inference**: This involves the ability to reason correctly and deduce truths based on one's understanding of the Dhamma and its consistency. It is demonstrated by the capacity to discern whether a statement "stand[s] against the suttas and tally[ies] with the Vinaya". If a teaching aligns with these established standards, it is understood as correct; otherwise, it is rejected. The Tathāgata's own answers to questions arise "on the spot" because of his "thorough penetration of the property of the Dhamma," indicating that deep understanding naturally leads to correct inference. This knowledge is rooted in perceiving "the consistency of the Dhamma".

3.  **Knowledge with regard to encompassing (the minds of others)**: This refers to a profound intuitive or even direct awareness of the mental states, thoughts, and intentions of others. The sources provide examples of this ability:
    *   The Blessed One discerning the "line of thinking in Venerable Anuruddha's awareness".
    *   The Blessed One "encompass[ing] the awareness of the entire assembly" to identify those capable of understanding the Dhamma.
    *   Brahmā Sahampati also possessing this ability, "knowing with his own awareness the line of thinking in [the Buddha's] awareness". This is explicitly named as one of the four knowledges in the Dasuttara Sutta.

4.  **Knowledge of conventions**: This is the understanding of societal norms, classifications, and linguistic constructs without being bound by them. The Buddha acknowledged commonly "agreed upon" realities by the wise, stating "Whatever is agreed upon by the wise as not existing in the world, of that I too say, 'It doesn't exist.' Whatever is agreed upon by the wise as existing in the world, of that I too say, 'It exists.'". This knowledge allows one to transcend "run-of-the-mill idiosyncratic truths" and theoretical judgments (such as those regarding the cosmos being eternal or finite, or the self existing after death) that lead to dispute. A sage who "doesn't speak of purity in connection with view" and "ponders dependencies" is released and "doesn't get into disputes".

These knowledges are not static but are qualities "to be made to arise", implying active cultivation and development. Their arising is intrinsically linked to the larger practice of the Dhamma. For instance, developing concentration helps one "discern in line with what has come into being," including the Four Noble Truths. Moreover, purifying one's virtue and making views straight serves as "the basis of skillful mental qualities" for developing core practices like the four establishings of mindfulness, which leads one "beyond Māra's realm". The seven factors for awakening—mindfulness, analysis of qualities, persistence, rapture, calm, concentration, and equanimity—are also crucial for the culmination of clear knowing and release.

### Resolution

The cultivation and arising of these four knowledges are fundamental to realizing the ultimate goal of the holy life. They are part of the path that the Tathāgata "rightly awakened to". The Dhamma, which encompasses these knowledges, is taught by the Blessed One for the **"purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow & lamentation, for the disappearance of pain & distress, for the attainment of the right method, & for the realization of unbinding"**.

Specifically, the teachings that are categorical, such as the Four Noble Truths—stress, its origination, cessation, and the path to its cessation—are declared because they are "conducive to the goal, conducive to the Dhamma, and basic to the holy life. They lead to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to stilling, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to unbinding". The ultimate aim of developing these knowledges, therefore, is to contribute to the "right ending of suffering & stress".

By making these knowledges arise, practitioners move towards a state of **complete release** from all defilements and attachments, ultimately realizing the unbinding property with "no fuel remaining".
