Venerable Sāriputta, in the DASUTTARA SUTTA, presents a detailed framework of Dhamma qualities, categorized by number, leading to unbinding and the end of suffering and stress. Within this framework, he identifies certain Dhamma qualities as "hard to penetrate," including four specific types of concentration.

Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of these four concentrations, presented in a Situation, Complication, and Resolution format:

### Situation

Venerable Sāriputta states that **four concentrations are hard to penetrate**: "concentration that has a share in decline, concentration that has a share in stability, concentration that has a share in distinction, [and] concentration that has a share in penetration". These concentrations are aspects of Dhamma that are described by the Tathāgata as "deep, hard to see, hard to realize, tranquil, refined, beyond the scope of conjecture, subtle, to-be-experienced by the wise".

### Complication

These four concentrations are considered "hard to penetrate" due to the inherent challenges in their development and understanding:

*   **Concentration that has a share in decline:** This type of concentration is challenging because, instead of leading to positive outcomes, it is associated with a decrease in skillful mental qualities and an increase in unskillful ones. A person might be "normally of an intensely passionate nature," or "aversive nature," or "deluded nature," perpetually experiencing pain and distress. Such a state signifies a lack of progress in one's practice, where the faculties of "conviction, persistence, mindfulness, concentration, [and] discernment—appear weakly". The presence of "five obstacles, hindrances that overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment," such as sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty, makes it "impossible" for one "without strength and weak in discernment" to "understand what is for his own benefit... [or] to realize a superior human state". This state of decline also indicates an inability to discern a quality's "share in decline as it has come to be". The Dhamma itself "is not easily realized by those overcome with aversion & passion," especially those "cloaked in the mass of darkness" who "won't see" what is "abstruse, subtle, deep, hard to see".

*   **Concentration that has a share in stability:** This refers to a state of concentration that maintains itself without necessarily leading to further advancement. While not declining, it also does not move towards higher states of realization. The difficulty here lies in recognizing and transcending this equilibrium if it does not lead to "penetration". It may represent a practitioner who is content with a certain level of peace or mental composure but lacks the deep insight required for ultimate liberation.

*   **Concentration that has a share in distinction:** This concentration indicates a stage of progress beyond mere stability, where the practitioner experiences qualitative improvements in their practice. "Distinction" implies a movement towards increasingly refined states of awareness and understanding. However, without fully discerning "this quality has a share in distinction as it has come to be," one may be incapable of attaining further profound realizations. For instance, the Dhamma includes the four jhāna states as "devotions to pleasure that lead exclusively to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, calming, direct knowledge, self-awakening, & unbinding". While these are distinguished states, moving through them to full release requires sustained effort and insight.

*   **Concentration that has a share in penetration:** This is the highest level, where concentration leads to direct and profound insight into the nature of phenomena. "Penetration" implies a deep understanding that brings one to the "immediacy that leads to the ending of the effluents". The difficulty here lies in cultivating the necessary faculties and overcoming remaining obstructions to achieve this deep seeing. The development of concentration leads to "the attainment of knowledge & vision" and "the ending of the effluents". A key aspect of penetration is the "knowledge of the unprovoked", which is itself a Dhamma that "should be made to arise", highlighting its advanced and subtle nature. Furthermore, individuals may face "kamma obstruction, a defilement obstruction, a result-of-(past)-kamma obstruction," or "lack conviction, no desire (to listen), and... dull discernment," which prevent them from "alighting on the lawfulness, the rightness of skillful qualities". Not listening well or not applying one's mind to gnosis also hinders this penetration. These deep Dhamma topics are also "beyond their range" for many, and only the Tathāgata, his disciples, or those who have heard from them can truly satisfy the mind with answers.

### Resolution

Successfully navigating and penetrating these concentrations leads to the ultimate goals of the holy life. When the mind is nurtured with discernment through concentration, it is "rightly released from the effluents, i e, the effluent of sensuality, the effluent of becoming, the effluent of ignorance". The teachings declared by the Blessed One as "categorical" are those that are "conducive to the goal, conducive to the Dhamma, and basic to the holy life," leading "to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to calm, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to unbinding". The result of such practice is "clear knowing & release", culminating in a state where one is "above, below, everywhere released" and "doesn't focus on 'I am this'". This ultimately means "birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for the sake of this world," leading to the state of an arahant and putting "an end to suffering & stress". Such an attainment is likened to "knowledge of the unprovoked," which is a Dhamma that "should be realized".
