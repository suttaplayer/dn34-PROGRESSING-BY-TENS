In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhammas, including qualities that are "on the side of distinction". Among these, he specifically highlights the "four unyokings: the unyoking of the yoke of sensuality, the unyoking of the yoke of becoming, the unyoking of the yoke of views, the unyoking of the yoke of ignorance". This statement encapsulates the central aim of the holy life: liberation from the bonds that tie beings to suffering.

Here is a detailed analysis of this statement in a Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) format:

### Situation: The Burden of Yokes

Beings in the world are typically bound by various "yokes" which prevent their liberation and lead to continued suffering. The four yokes are: **the yoke of sensuality, the yoke of becoming, the yoke of views, and the yoke of ignorance**. When one is "conjoined with evil, unskillful mental qualities," they are said "not to be at rest from the yoke," which results in suffering and further birth, aging, and death. These yokes function as "obstacles, hindrances that overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment". They make it impossible for a person to understand what is for their own benefit, the benefit of others, or the benefit of both, thereby hindering the realization of a superior human state or noble distinction in knowledge and vision.

### Complication: The Path to Unyoking

The process of "unyoking" from these powerful bonds is achieved through discerning and abandoning the underlying causes of entanglement.
*   **Unyoking from sensuality** involves discerning the "origination, the passing away, the allure, the drawbacks, & the escape from sensuality". When one thoroughly understands these aspects of sensuality, they are no longer "obsessed with sensual passion, sensual delight, sensual attraction, sensual infatuation, sensual thirst, sensual fever, sensual fascination, sensual craving". This is also related to abandoning "sensual desire," which is an obstacle that overwhelms awareness and weakens discernment.
*   **Unyoking from becoming** relates to abandoning the craving for becoming and non-becoming, which are among the three cravings that should be abandoned.
*   **Unyoking from views** necessitates the abandonment of various fixed and often conflicting viewpoints about the world and self. These can include views about the cosmos being eternal or not, finite or infinite, or the soul and body being the same or different. Such "view-standpoints" are not conducive to the goal, Dhamma, or holy life, and do not lead to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, calm, direct knowledge, self-awakening, or unbinding. A wise person, by contrast, "doesn't get into disputes" or theorized judgments, and seeks "inner peace" by "looking for what is ungrasped with regard to views".
*   **Unyoking from ignorance** involves overcoming the lack of knowledge and developing "discernment" or "clear knowing". Ignorance is identified as one of the three roots of what is unskillful, along with greed and aversion. This requires conscious effort, as "discernment does not develop" when defiled by ignorance.

The path to these unyokings is one of continuous effort and development, involving training oneself to be "peaceful in faculties & peaceful in hearts". It requires cultivating a mind that is **modest, content, reclusive, persistent, mindful, concentrated, and discerning**, and one that "enjoys non-objectification, who delights in non-objectification, not for one who enjoys & delights in objectification". The process is described as "going against the flow" of worldly desires and attachments.

### Resolution: The Attainment of Unbinding

The successful accomplishment of these unyokings leads to a profound state of **release and the ending of suffering and stress**. Those who are disjoined from all yokes are called "sages" who have "crossed over birth & aging". This state is characterized by:
*   **Awareness-release and discernment-release**, which arise "from the fading of passion" and "from the fading of ignorance," respectively.
*   The **abandonment of effluents**, with their root destroyed, made like a palmyra stump, "deprived of the conditions of development, not destined for future arising".
*   The monk who achieves this is said to have "cut through craving, has ripped off the fetter, and—from rightly breaking through conceit—has put an end to suffering & stress".
*   The ultimate goal is "unbinding" (Nibbāna), described as the "unexcelled safety from bonds" and a state "where there is nothing felt" yet is "pleasant".
*   Such a person is "unagitated" and "at peace".

The Dhamma taught by the Tathāgata, which includes these unyokings, is described as leading "to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to stilling, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to unbinding". This profound release is "hard to penetrate" for those who have not undertaken the necessary practice and cultivation.
