    > thanissaro bhikkhu has translated ĀSAVA as both effluents and fermentation. what is the cause for this dual translation and how should it be understood

Thanissaro Bhikkhu's dual translation of the Pali term **āsava** as both "effluents" and "fermentations" is a deliberate choice to capture the multifaceted meaning of the word. He uses these two English terms to convey different, yet complementary, aspects of the concept.

***

### The Meaning of Āsava

In a Buddhist context, **āsava** (Sanskrit: *āsrava*) refers to the deep-seated mental defilements that keep beings bound to the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra) and suffering (dukkha). They are the "outflows" of the mind that sustain its unwholesome tendencies. The three main āsavas are:

* **kāmāsava**: the effluent of sensual desire
* **bhavāsava**: the effluent of becoming or craving for existence
* **avijjāsava**: the effluent of ignorance

A fourth, *diṭṭhāsava* (the effluent of wrong views), is sometimes included as well. The destruction of these āsavas (*āsavakkhaya*) is synonymous with the attainment of enlightenment.

***

### Effluents: The Outflow of Defilement

The translation "effluents" is a literal rendering of the Pali root *su*, which means "to flow." This translation emphasizes the idea of something flowing out from the mind. Āsavas are seen as mental tendencies that "flow out" through the six senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the mind) and into the world, shaping our experience and perpetuating the cycle of cause and effect. They are the springs from which the currents of saṃsāra arise, like a river in a flood. This imagery highlights how these mental defilements are active, flowing processes that extend our suffering into our interactions with the world.

### Fermentations: The Intoxicating Process

The translation "fermentations" is based on the Pali word also being used to describe wine made from fermented fruit. This meaning highlights the intoxicating, corrupting, and festering quality of the āsavas. Like a substance that ferments, these mental defilements bubble up within the mind, creating a sense of intoxication that clouds our judgment and leads to unskillful actions. The idea of fermentation suggests a slow, seeping process that has been going on for a long time, gradually tainting the mind and making it difficult to see reality clearly. 

***

### Why the Dual Translation?

Thanissaro Bhikkhu's use of both terms is a way to avoid the limitations of a single English word. He recognizes that no one term can fully capture the complex, metaphorical richness of the original Pali.

* **Effluents** points to the active, outward-flowing nature of the defilements, showing how they influence our engagement with the world.
* **Fermentations** points to the inward, corrupting, and intoxicating nature of the defilements, showing how they fester within the mind itself.

By using both, he provides a more complete understanding of **āsava** as a process that is both internal and external, deeply rooted in the mind, and actively shaping our experience of reality. The two translations work together to illustrate the dynamic, powerful, and insidious nature of these mental defilements.

The term **āsava** can be interpreted as either an "inflow" or an "outflow," and Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation in the *Dīgha Nikāya 34* is an example of this dual nature.

***

### The Dual Meaning of Āsava

* **Outflow (Effluent):** This is the more common translation, which refers to the way mental defilements flow **out** from the mind to taint and corrupt our experience of the world. It suggests an active process of the mind projecting its unwholesome tendencies onto reality. This is the sense of "effluent" as a corrupting outflow.

* **Inflow (Influx):** The term can also be understood as an "inflow" or "influx," meaning something that flows **into** the mind, thereby polluting it. This is a common interpretation in ancient Buddhist commentaries, and it is also how the term is used in Jainism. The idea is that these defilements are like a leak in a container, allowing unwanted influences to seep in.

***

### Understanding the Context in DASUTTARA SUTTA

In the specific phrase from the *DASUTTARA SUTTA*, "Contact accompanied by effluents & subject to clinging," the word "effluents" (*āsava*) is paired with "clinging" (*upādāna*). Here's how this combination works:

* **Contact** (*phassa*) is the initial point where our senses meet an object. For example, when the eye sees a form, the ear hears a sound, etc.
* **The Inflow:** The "effluents" are the **unwholesome mental states** (sensual desire, craving for existence, and ignorance) that are already present in the mind. When a contact occurs, these effluents act like a pre-existing contamination. They flow **into** and color our perception of that contact, preventing a clear, unbiased experience.
* **The Outflow:** This colored, contaminated perception then leads to **clinging** (*upādāna*), which can be seen as the mind's clinging **outward** to the object of contact, solidifying the initial unwholesome reaction. The clinging then reinforces the effluents, creating a vicious cycle.

So, in this context, the effluents are identified as an **inflow** that taints the moment of contact, which then leads to an **outflow** of clinging. The dual translation reflects that the mental defilements of *āsava* are both a source of contamination that flows into the mind and a force that flows out, causing us to cling and perpetuate suffering. It is a subtle and sophisticated way of describing how the mind's unwholesome tendencies are both a cause and an effect in the process of perception and attachment.

### The Causal Chain

The statement "Contact accompanied by effluents & **subject to** clinging" outlines a specific causal process, which can be broken down as follows:

1.  **Initial Condition (Pre-existing state):** The mind is already permeated with **effluents** (*āsava*). These are the deep-seated defilements like sensual desire, craving for existence, and ignorance.
2.  **Activating Event:** **Contact** (*phassa*) occurs. This is the simple, neutral meeting of a sense organ (e.g., the eye) and its object (e.g., a visible form).
3.  **The Resultant Condition:** Because the mind is already "accompanied by effluents," this contact is not neutral. It is tainted by the unwholesome predispositions. This tainted contact is what makes it **"subject to clinging"** (*upādāniyaṃ*).
4.  **The Consequence:** The moment of contact, now contaminated by the effluents, becomes a fertile ground for the arising of **clinging** (*upādāna*). This isn't an automatic process but a potential one; the conditions are ripe for clinging to arise.

### Requisite Condition vs. Automatic Cause

The use of **"subject to"** is crucial because it distinguishes this relationship from a simple, one-to-one cause-and-effect relationship. It suggests that contact, when it occurs in a mind with un-eradicated effluents, becomes the requisite condition for clinging to arise. It's like a dry forest (the mind with effluents) being "subject to" a wildfire (clinging) if a single spark (contact) lands in it.

This highlights that the **effluents are the fundamental problem**. If the effluents were gone, contact would still occur, but it would no longer be "subject to clinging." It would simply be a neutral event, and the cycle of suffering would be broken. This is the state of an Arahant (an awakened one) who has destroyed the effluents. They experience contact, but it doesn't give rise to clinging, because the prerequisite condition for it is no longer present.

The core issue lies in the relationship between **fetters** (*saṃyojana*) and **clinging** (*upādāna*). They are not identical concepts, though they are deeply interconnected.

* **Fetter** is a more fundamental and deep-seated mental defilement. It's a chain that binds the mind to the cycle of *saṃsāra* (rebirth).
* **Clinging** is the active manifestation of that fetter. It's the process by which the mind seizes upon an object of attachment, driven by the underlying fetter.

Therefore, the destruction of a fetter implies the destruction of its associated clinging in a *fundamental* way, but this doesn't mean that the *process* of clinging disappears entirely. It means the **root cause** of that specific type of clinging is gone forever. The stream-enterer is no longer bound by these chains.
