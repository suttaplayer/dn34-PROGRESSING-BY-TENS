The statement "Which one dhamma should be abandoned? **The conceit 'I am'**" refers to a core teaching within the Dhamma regarding a fundamental defilement of the mind that must be overcome for liberation. This conceit is a significant obstacle on the path to unbinding.

**Situation**

The conceit "I am" is a singular quality (dhamma) that is explicitly identified as needing to be abandoned. It is described as "I-making or mine-making conceit-obsession" which can be directed toward the conscious body or all external themes. This "conceit as to 'I am'" is considered one of the seven obsessions. Uninstructed, run-of-the-mill persons tend to assume a self in various ways, often by assuming one or more of the five clinging-aggregates (form, feeling, perception, fabrications, consciousness) to be the self, or as something possessing the self, or within the self. Even a "stupid baby boy lying on its back" has a latent tendency to self-identification view, though the thought of "self-identification" may not yet occur to it. The presence of conceit can even be observed in the minds of the wise, such as Venerable Sāriputta, when he reflected on seeing the cosmos with the divine eye.

**Complication**

This "I-making or mine-making conceit-obsession" prevents a monk from entering and remaining in "awareness-release & discernment-release". It is directly linked to the continuation of suffering and stress; for one to put an end to suffering and stress, it requires "rightly breaking through conceit". The presence of conceit hinders spiritual progress, as "there's no taming for one fond of conceit, no sagacity for one unconcentrated," which means such a person "won't cross over beyond Deaths' realm". This conceit often manifests as a tendency to dispute, as "whoever supposes 'equal,' 'superior,' or 'inferior,' by that he'd dispute". The Dhamma is not easily realized by those whose minds are "overcome with aversion & passion," or "cloaked in the mass of darkness", and conceit is a defiling effluent. A person who has "consummate[d] in view" is described as incapable of giving rise to self-identity view, doubt, grasping at habits & practices, and passions that lead to lower realms, further underscoring the unskillful nature of conceit.

**Resolution**

The ultimate resolution for the "conceit 'I am'" is its abandonment, leading to the **ending of suffering and stress**. The sources offer a fascinating perspective on how this is achieved: "This body comes into being through conceit. And yet it is by relying on conceit that conceit is to be abandoned". This suggests that the very aspiration or drive, even if initially tinged with self-importance, can be channeled toward overcoming the conceit itself.

Central to abandoning conceit is the understanding and realization of **not-self**:
*   The fundamental truth is that "**All phenomena are not-self**".
*   This means seeing that form, feeling, perception, fabrications, and consciousness are "inconstant, stressful, [and] not-self," leading one to discern: "**This is not mine. This is not my self. This is not what I am**".
*   Cultivating the "**perception of not-self in what is stressful**" is of "great fruit, of great benefit," leading to a "footing in the deathless".
*   Monks are instructed to "abandon desire-passion" for "whatever is not-self".
*   The world itself is described as "empty of a self or of anything pertaining to a self".

By understanding and applying the not-self teaching, one can "give up obsession with conceit" and become "stilled". A monk who no longer focuses on "I am this" is "released" and "crosses the flood not crossed before, for the sake of no further becoming". When "disenchanted" with form, feeling, perception, fabrications, and consciousness, a disciple becomes "dispassionate," and "through dispassion, he is released". This also includes cultivating "non-objectification" where the mind "leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, & released in the cessation of objectification". The goal is to develop a "right view" by discerning "what is unskillful" and its root, as well as "what is skillful" and its root. Ultimately, the Tathāgata's teaching aims for the abandonment of all defiling effluents, leading to "total unbinding through lack of clinging or sustenance," where their root is "destroyed, made like a palmyra stump, deprived of the conditions of development, not destined for future arising". This journey requires diligent self-care and continuous effort to "settle himself in what is correct".
