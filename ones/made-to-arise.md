In the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Venerable Sāriputta presents a framework of Dhammas that progress by tens, within which "knowledge of the unprovoked" or "unprovoked release of awareness" is identified as a dhamma that "should be made to arise" and "should be realized". This concept refers to a state of profound insight and liberation that is not conditioned or brought about by external stimuli or internal mental constructs.

**Situation**

The human experience is largely characterized by suffering and stress, arising from phenomena that are "fabricated" or "provoked" by various conditions. Ordinary beings perceive the world and its phenomena through "perceptions & categories of objectification," which in turn generates a cycle of obsessions including passion, resistance, views, uncertainty, conceit, and ignorance. These obsessions lead to "taking up rods & bladed weapons, of arguments, quarrels, disputes, accusations, divisive speech, & false speech," causing suffering that "cease without remainder" only when these objectifications are relinquished. Beings are described as "beset by birth, aging, & death; by sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, & despairs", continuously "transmigrating & wandering on" due to being "hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving". Furthermore, "unskillful thinking," such as that "imbued with sensuality… ill-will… [or] harmfulness," produces "blindness," "lack of vision," and "lack of knowledge," ultimately preventing the realization of unbinding. Without understanding causality and what should or should not be done, one dwells "bewildered & unprotected". The "uninstructed run-of-the-mill person" often fails to discern the origination, cessation, and path related to various views, leading those views to persist and grow.

**Complication**

This profound "knowledge of the unprovoked" or "unprovoked release of awareness" is described as "deep, hard to see, hard to realize, peaceful, refined, beyond the scope of conjecture, subtle, to-be-experienced by the wise". It is not readily attained by those "overcome with aversion & passion" or those "cloaked in the mass of darkness". Conventional debates and reliance on "theorized judgments" can lead to contention and further entanglement, as they often cling to "provoked" or classified phenomena. Even when the Dhamma is taught, a "poor grasp" of its meaning can lead to misrepresentation and suffering, as exemplified by the monk Sāti, who slandered the teaching and caused "much demerit for himself". The path to "unprovoked knowledge" requires a letting go of clinging, even to "pure" and "bright" views, as such clinging still prevents the full understanding of the Dhamma as being for "crossing over, not for holding on to".

**Resolution**

The "knowledge of the unprovoked" is ultimately the Buddha's own "unprovoked release," signifying "the last birth" and "no further becoming". The Dhamma is taught precisely for this purpose: "to lead to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to stilling, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to unbinding".

Attaining this knowledge involves a radical shift away from "I-making or mine-making conceit-obsession" concerning both one's physical body and all external themes, leading to the cutting through of craving and the ripping off of fetters, thereby ending suffering and stress. Through the "ending of the effluents," one "enters & remains in the effluent-free awareness-release & discernment-release, having directly known and realized them for himself right in the here & now".

The path to this state is one of active development and practice:
*   It requires **cultivating disenchantment** with "form, feeling, perception, fabrications, [and] consciousness" by focusing on their "inconstancy," "stress," and "not-self" nature. As one comprehends these aspects, one becomes "totally released" from suffering and stress.
*   A monk must be "discerning, endowed with discernment of arising & passing away—noble, penetrating, leading to the right ending of stress".
*   The "four establishings of mindfulness" are described as "the direct path for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow & lamentation, for the disappearance of pain & distress, for the attainment of the right method, & for the realization of unbinding".
*   A "concentrated monk discerns in line with what has come into being," which includes discerning "stress," its "origination," "cessation," and the "path of practice leading to the cessation of stress".
*   The Dhamma is "for one who enjoys non-objectification, who delights in non-objectification, not for one who enjoys & delights in objectification", emphasizing the letting go of mental constructions.

The ultimate outcome is the "total fading & cessation of ignorance, of this mass of darkness," leading to a "peaceful state, this exquisite state: the pacification of all fabrications; the relinquishing of all acquisitions; the ending of craving; dispassion; cessation; unbinding". This profound realization is often described as the arising of a "dustless, stainless Dhamma eye" where one sees that "Whatever is subject to origination is all subject to cessation". Through this comprehensive knowledge and release, one reaches "the end of stress".
