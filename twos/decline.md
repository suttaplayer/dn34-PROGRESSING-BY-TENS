The statement from the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34) highlights two qualities that are "on the side of decline": **being hard to instruct** and **evil friendship**. This refers to factors that hinder a person's spiritual progress and can lead to a regression in skillful qualities.

Here is a detailed analysis of this statement presented in a Situation, Complication, and Resolution (SCR) format:

### Situation: The Tendency Towards Decline

The core situation is the potential for individuals to decline in their spiritual development, moving away from skillful qualities towards unskillful ones. This decline can manifest in various ways and indicates a movement away from the path to liberation.

*   A person is considered to be "declining in skillful qualities" when there is an **increase in passion, aversion, and delusion**, and their "eye of discernment makes no headway in terms of deep possibilities & impossibilities".
*   Specific behaviors that lead to a monk's "falling away" include indulging in excessive activity, chatter, and sleep, indicating a lack of serious engagement with the holy life.
*   The sources suggest that the "true Dhamma" itself can disappear due to the actions of "worthless people who arise right here [within the Saṅgha]". This highlights that decline is not just an individual phenomenon but can also affect the community and the preservation of teachings.

### Complication: The Hindrances to Progress

The statement identifies "being hard to instruct" and "evil friendship" as key factors contributing to this decline. These qualities act as significant impediments to a person's ability to understand, practice, and realize the Dhamma.

**Being Hard to Instruct**: This refers to a lack of receptiveness and an unwillingness to learn or be corrected, which directly impedes spiritual growth.

*   Monks who are "hard to correct" and "lack endurance" and "don't take instruction respectfully" contribute to the "confusion & disappearance of the True Dhamma". Their resistance prevents them from internalizing the teachings necessary for progress.
*   Such individuals may exhibit traits like being **deceitful, stubborn, talkers, frauds, arrogant, and uncentered**, which prevents them from achieving "growth, increase, or abundance" in the Dhamma. Stubbornness, in particular, blocks the absorption of new understanding.
*   A "dull & exceedingly stupid" contemplative or brahman, when questioned, may resort to "verbal contortions" or "eel-wriggling" because they "don't discern as it has come to be that 'This is skillful,' or that 'This is unskillful'". This inability or unwillingness to engage directly with truth makes them uninstructable.
*   A "lack of conviction, stinginess, & laziness" can also make one "incapable of abandoning restlessness, abandoning a lack of restraint, abandoning poor virtue," further illustrating the internal states that contribute to being hard to instruct.
*   When a person grasps the Dhamma incorrectly, they not only slander the teachings but also "injur[e] yourself and accumulat[e] much demerit for yourself," leading to "long-term harm & suffering". This emphasizes the danger of intellectual inflexibility and arrogance.

**Evil Friendship**: This refers to associating with individuals who lead one away from wholesome states and towards unskillful actions, undermining one's spiritual efforts.

*   When "the wicked are dear to him" and "the good he doesn't hold dear," and he "approves of the ideas of the wicked," this is explicitly identified as "the way leading to decline". The influence of such friends fosters unwholesome tendencies.
*   The Blessed One explicitly advises, "**Don't associate with bad friends. Don't associate with the low**", indicating the inherent danger in such relationships.
*   A powerful simile illustrates this danger: "Just as one riding a small wooden plank would sink in the great sea, so does even one of right living sink, associating with the lazy. So avoid the lazy, those with low persistence". This highlights the corrupting influence and the ease with which one can be dragged down by negative associations.
*   An extreme example is Devadatta, who, "conquered by friendship with evil people," was "incurably doomed to deprivation, to hell, for the duration of an eon". This shows the severe consequences of associating with those who encourage unskillful actions.
*   Furthermore, if one "censures right view" and instead "honor[s] any contemplatives & brahmans who are of wrong view," this indicates a dangerous alignment with teachings that deviate from the Dhamma.

### Resolution: Cultivating the Path to Distinction

The antidote to decline, and the means to progress towards distinction, involves actively cultivating the opposite qualities: being easy to instruct and fostering admirable friendship.

**Being Easy to Instruct**: This denotes openness, humility, and a genuine desire to learn and apply the Dhamma.

*   When monks are "easy to correct" and "show endurance and take instruction respectfully," this leads to the "stability, non-confusion, & non-disappearance of the True Dhamma". Such receptiveness ensures the teachings are preserved and flourish.
*   The ability to "alight on the orderliness, on the rightness of skillful qualities" when listening to the Dhamma depends on being "discerning, neither dim-witted nor a drooling idiot," and not assuming understanding when one doesn't. This underscores the importance of a humble and attentive mind for instruction.
*   A "wise person" is recognized by their ability to "formulate a question appropriately," "answer a question appropriately," and "approve[] of" others' appropriate answers, demonstrating a constructive and open approach to learning and discussion.

**Admirable Friendship**: This involves seeking out and maintaining relationships with individuals who are virtuous, supportive, and conducive to spiritual growth.

*   The direct advice is to "**Associate with admirable friends. Associate with the best**". These associations are vital for spiritual health.
*   Admirable friends are characterized by their willingness to "give[] what is hard to give," "do[] what is hard to do," and "endure[] what is hard to endure", demonstrating their commitment and reliability in supporting one's practice.
*   "Association with people of integrity" is explicitly identified as a "factor for stream entry," alongside listening to the True Dhamma, appropriate attention, and practice in accordance with the Dhamma. This establishes admirable friendship as a foundational element for significant spiritual progress.
*   A monk who is a "friend to admirable people"—being "reverential, respectful," and "doing what his friends advise"—will, by being "mindful, alert," "attain[] step by step the ending of all fetters". This shows how good friendships actively support the path to liberation.
*   Ultimately, "the wise would associate with those who are" good, because "the good help you reach a good destination," while those who are not good "lead you to hell". The choice of friends has direct consequences for one's spiritual trajectory.
*   To avoid decline and achieve greatness, one should "respect the true Dhamma, recollecting the Buddhas' Teaching". This overarching commitment to the Dhamma and its principles underpins both receptiveness to instruction and the cultivation of beneficial relationships.
