In the Dasuttara Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhammas, categorized by numerical groupings (ones, twos, tens, etc.) that are beneficial, to be developed, comprehended, and so forth. While the specific phrase "Mindfulness & alertness" is not listed as a singular or explicitly numbered Dhamma category within the "progressing by tens" section of the Dasuttara Sutta, the concepts of mindfulness and alertness are fundamental and interwoven throughout the teachings, appearing as crucial elements in various contexts across the sources for the development of skillful qualities and the realization of unbinding. The essence of this combined concept is highly valued and frequently instructed by the Blessed One and his disciples.

Here is a detailed analysis of "Mindfulness & alertness" presented in a "Situation, Complication, Resolution" (SCR) format, drawing on relevant information from the sources:

### Situation

The practice of mindfulness and alertness arises from recognizing the inherent nature and challenges of existence.

*   The mind is described as **luminous, but it is defiled by incoming defilements**. The goal is for it to be freed from these defilements.
*   The fundamental causes for the origination of actions are **greed, aversion, and delusion**. These unskillful qualities contribute to suffering and stress, making the cultivation of opposing skillful qualities essential.
*   The human body is inherently **"afflicted, weak, & encumbered,"** and true health in this physical form is transient. This necessitates a focus on cultivating an unafflicted mind.
*   The world is frequently depicted as being "on fire with aging & death," highlighting the urgency for practice and salvaging oneself through virtuous actions.

### Complication

Without the cultivation of mindfulness and alertness, various hindrances and undesirable states manifest, preventing progress on the path.

*   An **"uninstructed run-of-the-mill person doesn't discern"** the luminous nature of the mind and how it becomes defiled, leading to no development of the mind.
*   When the mind is **"defiled by passion"** it is not released, and **"defiled by ignorance, discernment does not develop"**. This indicates that these defilements prevent deeper insight and liberation.
*   An "assembly trained in bombast" fails to deeply grasp the discourses of the Tathāgata, even those "deep, deep in their meaning, transcendent, connected with emptiness," because they "don't listen, don't lend ear, don't set their hearts on knowing them". This illustrates a lack of attentive engagement, which mindfulness and alertness counteract.
*   An imbalance in mental development, such as attending "solely to the theme of concentration," can lead the mind to **laziness**, while focusing only on "uplifted energy" can lead to **restlessness**. Exclusive attention to "equanimity" might prevent the mind from being "rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents".
*   For those who have "gone forth in this well-taught Dhamma & Vinaya," to **remain lazy & heedless "would not be fitting,"** implying that a lack of diligence is counterproductive to the holy life.
*   The **True Dhamma "does not last long"** after the Tathāgata's total unbinding if the "four establishings of mindfulness are neglected". This underscores their vital role in preserving and sustaining the teachings.

### Resolution

The consistent development of mindfulness and alertness is the direct path to inner peace, clarity, and ultimate liberation.

*   The practice of mindfulness and alertness is proclaimed as **"the direct path for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow & lamentation, for the disappearance of pain & distress, for the attainment of the right method, & for the realization of unbinding—in other words, the four establishings of mindfulness"**.
*   **Mindfulness** is cultivated by remaining **"focused on the body in & of itself—ardent, alert, & mindful—subduing greed & distress with reference to the world. He remains focused on feelings… mind… mental qualities in & of themselves—ardent, alert, & mindful—subduing greed & distress with reference to the world."** This is how a monk is mindful.
*   **Alertness** involves recognizing feelings, thoughts, and perceptions "as they arise, known as they persist, known as they subside." This ensures one is keenly aware of mental phenomena in real-time.
*   A practitioner should be **"possessed of mindfulness & alertness"** in all daily activities: "When going forward and returning, we will act with alertness. When looking toward and looking away… when bending and extending our limbs… when carrying our outer cloak, upper robe, & bowl… when eating, drinking, chewing, & tasting… when urinating & defecating… when walking, standing, sitting, falling asleep, waking up, talking, & remaining silent, we will act with alertness".
*   An astute monk "keeps his persistence aroused for abandoning unskillful qualities and taking on skillful qualities. He is steadfast, solid in his effort, not shirking his duties with regard to skillful qualities". This persistence is deeply supported by sustained mindfulness and alertness.
*   To attain right view, a disciple of the noble ones must discern **"what is unskillful, discerns the root of what is unskillful, discerns what is skillful, and discerns the root of what is skillful"**. Mindfulness and alertness facilitate this discerning process.
*   Mindfulness is explicitly identified as one of the **"seven factors for awakening"** alongside analysis of qualities, persistence, rapture, calm, concentration, and equanimity. These factors collectively lead to awakening.
*   When one develops "immeasurable concentration" while **"mindful & astute,"** realizations arise, such as knowing consciously when one enters and emerges from concentration.
*   Through the cultivation of mindfulness and alertness, and consequently **"lack of clinging or sustenance,"** consciousness becomes "neither externally scattered & diffused, nor internally positioned," leading to an unagitated state where **"there is no seed for the conditions of future birth, aging, death, or stress"**.
*   The ultimate aim of cultivating these qualities is to reach the supreme goal of the holy life, where **"Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for the sake of this world,"** leading to the state of an Arahant. Furthermore, the mind, nurtured with discernment, becomes "rightly released from the effluents, i e, the effluent of sensuality, the effluent of becoming, the effluent of ignorance".
