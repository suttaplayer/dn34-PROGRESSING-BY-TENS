The statement you've highlighted from the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Dīgha Nikāya 34, outlines seven "untrue dhammas" that contribute to a monk's decline: being without conviction, shame, compunction, learning, being lazy, having muddled mindfulness, and being undiscerning. This framework reveals crucial aspects of spiritual stagnation and failure within the Dhamma and Vinaya.

Here's a detailed analysis of this statement using the Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) format:

**Situation**
The Blessed One delineates "seven untrue dhammas" that are firmly "on the side of decline" for a monk. These qualities describe a state of being that impedes spiritual growth and can lead to a reversal of progress in the holy life. They include being **without conviction**, **without a sense of shame**, **without compunction**, **without learning**, being **lazy**, having **muddled mindfulness**, and being **undiscerning**. These are qualities that, if present, signify a person who is "incapable of touching superlative self-awakening".

**Complication**
The presence of these "untrue dhammas" creates significant obstacles to a monk's spiritual journey:

*   **Without conviction (assaḍdha):** A person lacking conviction is described as being "incapable of alighting on the orderliness, on the rightness of skillful qualities" even when listening to the true Dhamma. This absence of conviction is explicitly linked to the **food for ignorance** and is a quality of "people of no integrity". Such an individual is among the "seven untrue dhammas" that lead to decline.
*   **Without a sense of shame (ahirika) & without compunction (anottappī):** These two qualities, when absent, are considered "downward-leading qualities that tend to the confusion and disappearance of the true Dhamma". A monk who is without shame and compunction is **incapable of attaining arahantship, the foremost state**, or touching "superlative self-awakening". They are also identified as qualities that accompany ignorance as a "leader in the attainment of unskillful qualities".
*   **Without learning (assuta):** A lack of learning means monks may **learn suttas poorly memorized**, leading to meanings being "wrongly inferred," contributing to the "confusion & disappearance of the True Dhamma". Furthermore, they may not "attentively teach the discourses to others," causing the Dhamma to be "cut off at the root, left without an arbitrator" when they pass away. Not knowing fords means one doesn't question learned monks or resolve doubts.
*   **Lazy (kusīta) or low in persistence:** Laziness is a quality of "a person of no integrity" and is directly associated with being **"incapable of touching superlative self-awakening"**. It means shirking the duties of solitude and "not making an effort for the attaining of the as-yet-unattained, the reaching of the as-yet-unreached, the realization of the as-yet-unrealized".
*   **One of muddled mindfulness (muṭṭhassati):** If one's "mindfulness is muddled," it leads to one being "unalert, uncentered, his mind scattered, & his faculties uncontrolled," thus failing to fulfill the purpose of the contemplative life and being incapable of "touching superlative self-awakening". Such a state prevents the "arising of mindfulness" for those who pass away with it.
*   **Undiscerning (duppañña):** Being undiscerning means one is "dim-witted, a drooling idiot" and, "not understanding, one assumes one understands". Such a person is "without strength and weak in discernment," making it "impossible" for them to understand what is for their own benefit or for the benefit of others, or to realize "a superior human state".

**Resolution**
Conversely, cultivating the opposite of these "untrue dhammas"—namely, conviction, a sense of shame, compunction, learning, aroused persistence, established mindfulness, and discernment—leads to growth, stability, and ultimately, unbinding.

*   **Conviction (saddhā):** A monk with conviction is "convinced of the Tathāgata's awakening". This conviction acts as a "foundation post," enabling the abandonment of unskillful qualities and the development of skillful ones. It is considered one of the "seven noble treasures".
*   **A sense of shame (hiri) & compunction (ottappa):** These are vital "qualities that lead to a monk's non-decline" and are among the "seven true dhammas". They are also listed as "noble treasures", crucial for flourishing in the holy life and preventing "further becoming".
*   **Learning (bāhussuta):** A monk who has "heard much, has retained what he has heard, has stored what he has heard" is well-prepared to discern the Dhamma's meaning and purpose. Such a monk is able to "cross-question" and "dissect" the Dhamma, making "open what isn't open" and dispelling doubt. This leads to the "stability, non-confusion, & non-disappearance of the True Dhamma".
*   **Aroused persistence (āraddhaviriya):** This is essential for "abandoning unskillful mental qualities and taking on skillful mental qualities," being "steadfast, solid in his effort, not shirking his duties". Such a monk is "capable of touching superlative self-awakening".
*   **Established mindfulness (upaṭṭhitassati):** A monk with established mindfulness is "endowed with excellent proficiency in mindfulness, remembering & able to call to mind even things that were done & said long ago". This allows for a "pleasant abiding" and is one of the "factors for awakening". It is a critical component for "purification of beings" and "realization of unbinding".
*   **Discerning (paññavā):** A discerning monk is "endowed with discernment of arising & passing away—noble, penetrating, leading to the right ending of stress". This quality is fundamental to the holy life, leading to the "increase, plenitude, development, & culmination" of discernment. It empowers one to discern stress and its cessation. Being discerning is also a "noble treasure", and when well shored-up by discernment, a monk's mind is "devoid of passion, aversion, and delusion" and can affirm that "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done".

By abandoning these "untrue dhammas" and cultivating their counterparts, a monk moves from a state of decline towards growth, insight, and ultimately, liberation from suffering and stress.
