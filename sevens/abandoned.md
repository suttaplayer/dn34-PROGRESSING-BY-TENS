Venerable Sāriputta, in the DASUTTARA SUTTA, outlines specific categories of Dhammas, including those that should be abandoned. Among these are the "seven obsessions," which are presented as crucial for a monk's spiritual progress.

**Situation**

The seven obsessions that should be abandoned are: **sensual passion, resistance, views, uncertainty, conceit, passion for becoming, and ignorance**. These are considered internal "stains," "enemies," "foes," "murderers," and "adversaries". The Dhamma teaches that if the mind is **defiled by passion, it is not released**, and if defiled by ignorance, discernment does not develop. These obsessions are identified as hindrances that **overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment**.

**Complication**

If these obsessions are not abandoned, they lead to significant suffering and impediment on the path to liberation.
*   **Passion, aversion, and delusion are primary causes for the origination of unskillful actions**. An individual overcome with passion wills for their own detriment, the detriment of others, or both, and experiences mental stress and sorrow.
*   When one does not abandon hindrances such as sensual desire, ill will, restlessness, anxiety, and uncertainty (which align with some obsessions), it becomes **impossible to understand what is for one's own benefit** or to realize a superior human state.
*   Holding wrong views or lacking a clear understanding can lead to bewilderment and a lack of desire or effort to determine what should or shouldn't be done, as seen in those who "fall back on lack of cause and lack of condition as being essential".
*   An example of the severe consequences of not abandoning these qualities is seen in Devadatta, who, "conquered by evil desires—his mind overwhelmed—is incurably doomed to deprivation, to hell, for the duration of an eon".
*   **Ignorance** is described as "a great delusion whereby they have wandered-on a long, long time", indicating its profound role in perpetuating suffering.
*   More generally, if there is delight, welcome, or clinging to the causes by which "perceptions & categories of objectification assail a person," then **these obsessions will continue to exist**, leading to arguments, quarrels, disputes, and false speech.

**Resolution**

The abandonment and destruction of these seven obsessions are essential for fulfilling the holy life and achieving liberation.
*   From the **fading of passion, there is awareness-release**, and from the **fading of ignorance, there is discernment-release**.
*   When passion is abandoned, a person no longer wills for detriment to self or others and does not experience mental stress or sorrow.
*   The Dhamma itself is taught for the purpose of abandoning passion, aversion, and delusion. Those who have abandoned these, with their roots destroyed and deprived of conditions for future arising, are called "Well-Gone".
*   **Conceit** is specifically addressed; abandoning "I-making or my-making conceit-obsession" leads to cutting through craving, ripping off fetters, and putting an end to suffering and stress. By "abandoning conceit, without conceit," a monk can become "totally unbound". One should "give up obsession with conceit" to become "stilled".
*   For **sensual passion**, practices like "focusing on foulness in the body" can lead to its abandonment, allowing a monk to become "released" and "at peace". Furthermore, one should "subdue greed for sensuality, & see renunciation as rest".
*   The development of discernment is key, leading one to see that "All fabrications are inconstant" and "All phenomena are not-self," which fosters disenchantment with stress and leads to purity.
*   The path to abandon these obsessions and end suffering is the **noble eightfold path**, which includes right view, right resolve, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration.
*   A monk should "investigate in such a way that, his consciousness neither externally scattered & diffused, nor internally positioned," so that "from lack of clinging or sustenance he would be unagitated," thereby eliminating the "seed for the conditions of future birth, aging, death, or stress". This cultivation ultimately leads to a state where one "enjoys non-objectification" and "delights in non-objectification".
