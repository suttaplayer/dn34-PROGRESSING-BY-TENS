Here is an analysis of the statement from the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), presented in a "Situation, Complication, Resolution" format.

**Situation**

The DASUTTARA SUTTA presents a comprehensive framework of Dhamma teachings, categorized by numerical sets. Within this framework, Venerable Sāriputta explicitly identifies **seven true dhammas** as being "on the side of distinction". These qualities are: **conviction, a sense of shame, compunction, learning, aroused persistence, established mindfulness, and discerning**. These seven qualities are contrasted with "seven untrue dhammas" that are "on the side of decline," which include being without conviction, shame, compunction, learning, and being lazy, of muddled truth, and undiscerning.

**Complication**

The absence or neglect of these true dhammas leads to significant obstacles and negative outcomes in one's spiritual journey. For instance, without abandoning "impurities" such as being unvirtuous, envious, or stingy, one can be "placed in hell". Five "hindrances"—sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty—can "overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment," making it impossible to "realize a superior human state". Furthermore, if the "four establishings of mindfulness are neglected," then the "noble eightfold path leading to the right ending of suffering & stress is neglected". Similarly, monks who are "deceitful, stubborn, talkers, frauds, arrogant, & uncentered" are described as "not followers" of the teachings and attain "no growth, increase, or abundance" in the Dhamma-Vinaya. These negative traits and omissions illustrate the "complication" that these true dhammas are designed to address.

**Resolution**

The cultivation of these seven true dhammas facilitates profound progress and leads to liberation from suffering:

*   **Conviction (Saddhā):** This refers to having a deep-seated belief and confidence in the Tathāgata's awakening and his teachings. This conviction serves as a "foundation post" and is considered a "treasure". It is expected that a disciple with conviction will "keep his persistence aroused for abandoning unskillful mental qualities and taking on skillful mental qualities". When a disciple's mind "heads straight, based on the Tathāgata," they gain "a sense of the goal, gains a sense of the Dhamma, gains joy connected with the Dhamma".
*   **A sense of Shame (Hiri):** This quality involves feeling internal shame at the thought of engaging in bodily, verbal, or mental misconduct. Those "restrained by shame" are considered "rare" and are "always mindful". It is an essential quality that helps prevent decline and is recognized as a "treasure".
*   **Compunction (Ottappa):** Often paired with shame, compunction refers to the dread of the negative consequences of unskillful actions. It also functions as a "treasure" and a quality that leads to non-decline.
*   **Learning (Bahussuta):** This entails having "heard much, retained what he has heard, has stored what he has heard" from the Dhamma, along with discussing, accumulating, and examining it well. Listening to the Dhamma in this way offers rewards such as hearing "what one has not heard before," clarifying previous understanding, getting rid of doubt, straightening one's views, and making one's mind serene. A learned monk discerns the Dhamma's "higher meaning or goal".
*   **Aroused Persistence (Āraddha-vīriya):** This means keeping one's "persistence aroused for abandoning unskillful qualities and taking on skillful qualities," being "steadfast, solid in his effort, not shirking his duties". Such persistence is crucial for releasing an "unreleased mind" and for the "ending of the effluents". The Dhamma is specifically "for one whose persistence is aroused, not for one who is lazy".
*   **Established Mindfulness (Upaṭṭhita-sati):** This quality is characterized by being "mindful, endowed with excellent proficiency in mindfulness, remembering & able to call to mind even things that were done & said long ago". It is described as "the direct path for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow & lamentation, for the disappearance of pain & distress, for the attainment of the right method, & for the realization of unbinding—in other words, the four establishings of mindfulness". When cultivated, mindfulness contributes to the "seven factors for awakening".
*   **Discerning (Paññā):** A discerning monk is "endowed with discernment of arising & passing away—noble, penetrating, leading to the right ending of stress". Discernment, when "nurtured with concentration," yields "great fruit, great reward," leading to a mind "rightly released from the effluents". A wise person is capable of formulating and answering questions appropriately. This quality is fundamental to knowing and seeing, which leads to "the ending of effluents".

The collective development and pursuit of these "seven true dhammas" ensure the "stability, non-confusion, & non-disappearance of the True Dhamma". Ultimately, a monk whose mind is "well shored-up by discernment" through these practices can rightly declare, "'Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world'". If monks cultivate these qualities and "dwell rightly, this world will not be empty of arahants".
