Here is an analysis of the statement from the DASUTTARA SUTTA, presented in the Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) format, drawing upon the provided sources:

**Situation: The Aspirational Monk**

A monk who is "a person of integrity" or "deserving of praise" demonstrates a profound and unwavering commitment to the practice of the Dhamma. This aspirational state is characterized by "keen desire" for, and a sustained "love for," several key aspects of the holy life.

Specifically, such a monk:
*   Has a keen desire to **undertake the training** and does not abandon this commitment. This training encompasses heightened virtue, heightened mind, and heightened discernment.
*   Shows a keen desire to **attend carefully to the Dhamma**. This involves hearing, retaining, discussing, accumulating, examining, and well-penetrating the teachings in terms of their views, ensuring they are admirable in the beginning, middle, and end, and proclaim a perfectly pure holy life. To truly attend to the Dhamma, one must listen without contempt for the talk, the speaker, or oneself, and with an unscattered, gathered mind, demonstrating discernment.
*   Possesses a keen desire to **subdue his wants**. This is reflected in being easy to support, easily contented with life's requisites, and having few duties or projects. It is also about avoiding self-aggrandizement and being content with any old robe cloth, almsfood, lodging, or medicinal requisites.
*   Has a keen desire to **find solitude**. This means not neglecting seclusion and dedicating oneself to the internal tranquility of awareness.
*   Arouses **persistence**. This involves being steadfast and solid in effort, consistently abandoning unskillful qualities, and taking on skillful ones without shirking duties.
*   Is **mindful and adept**. This means being mindful, having excellent proficiency in mindfulness, and being able to recall things done and said long ago. It implies being alert and aware of feelings, thoughts, and perceptions as they arise, persist, and subside.
*   Has a keen desire to **penetrate (right) view**. This is about being discerning, endowed with a noble and penetrating discernment of arising and passing away, which leads to the right ending of stress.

**Complication: Obstacles to the Aspirational State**

Maintaining these qualities and progressing in the Dhamma is not without challenges. These difficulties often arise from unskillful mental qualities and a lack of diligent practice:
*   **Hindrances**: Sensual desire, ill will, sloth and drowsiness, restlessness and anxiety, and uncertainty are described as obstacles that "overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment". Without abandoning these, understanding one's own benefit or the benefit of others, or realizing a superior human state, is impossible.
*   **Laziness and Heedlessness**: Remaining lazy and heedless, even after having gone forth into the well-taught Dhamma and Vinaya, is considered unfitting. A holy life is fruitless if unskillful mental qualities increase while skillful ones decline. A monk consumed by sloth, delighting in chatter, or restlessness is incapable of achieving superlative self-awakening.
*   **Defilements and Biases**: The path to release requires abandoning fundamental defilements like passion, aversion, and delusion. An arahant monk, for example, cannot possibly follow biases based on desire, aversion, fear, or delusion. These underlying issues act as obstacles for anyone on the path.

**Resolution: The Fruits of Diligent Practice**

When a monk maintains and cultivates these "seven grounds for praise" with heedfulness and persistence, they pave the way for profound realizations and the ultimate cessation of suffering:
*   **Purification and Unbinding**: Developing and pursuing the "four establishings of mindfulness"—focusing on the body, feelings, mind, and mental qualities—is the "direct path for the purification of beings," leading to the overcoming of sorrow and lamentation, and the "realization of unbinding".
*   **Jhānas and Release**: Through diligent practice, a monk can attain the four jhānas, which are heightened mental states and pleasant abidings. Ultimately, this leads to entering and remaining in the "effluent-free awareness-release & discernment-release," having directly known and realized them in the here and now.
*   **Progress on the Path**: Such a monk gains deep understanding, for instance, discerning stress, its origination, cessation, and the path to its cessation. This persistent effort allows them to become "fulfilled & fully accomplished in this Dhamma & Vinaya—the ultimate person".
*   **End of Suffering**: The culmination of this path is the eradication of suffering. As a monk's mind is "well shored-up by discernment," they can affirm that "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world". This release stems from becoming "disenchanted with all fabrications," becoming "dispassionate," and finally being "released" from the cycle of stress.
*   **The Nature of the Holy Life**: The holy life itself is described as lived "with training as a reward, with discernment as its surpassing state, with release as its heartwood, and with mindfulness as its governing principle". This illustrates how the "seven grounds for praise" are integral to the very essence and purpose of the spiritual journey.
