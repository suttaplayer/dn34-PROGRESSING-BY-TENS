Venerable Sāriputta presents a framework of Dhammas in the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), including a set of seven perceptions that "should be made to arise". These perceptions are the perception of **inconstancy**, the perception of **not-self**, the perception of **unattractiveness**, the perception of **drawbacks**, the perception of **abandoning**, the perception of **dispassion**, and the perception of **cessation**. Developing and pursuing these perceptions is described as having "great fruit" and "great benefit," gaining a "footing in the deathless," and having "the deathless as their final end".

Here is a detailed analysis of this statement using the Situation, Complication, and Resolution (SCR) framework:

### Situation

The common human experience is one of entanglement and ongoing suffering, rooted in a fundamental misunderstanding of reality. Individuals often perceive the world and themselves in ways that lead to attachment and agitation. An uninstructed person tends to assume forms, feelings, perceptions, fabrications, or consciousness to be "self," or "mine," or "what I am". This grasping is a primary cause for the origination of stress, leading to birth, aging, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair. They delight in sensuality and objectification, unknowingly perpetuating their own distress. This adherence to views and craving keeps beings bound, preventing them from going beyond transmigration and the planes of deprivation. Without comprehending reality as it truly is, beings take a stand on signs and become bound by death.

### Complication

The failure to develop these crucial perceptions means remaining trapped in this cycle of suffering. Without recognizing the true nature of phenomena, individuals are perpetually susceptible to craving, attachment, and renewed becoming. The Dhamma taught by the Blessed One aims to counter these deeply ingrained patterns of perception and clinging. The danger of not developing these perceptions is that one's unskillful mental qualities increase, while skillful qualities decline, leading to a state of decline. If these perceptions are not cultivated, the mind remains obscured by defilements like passion, aversion, and delusion, which are described as causes for the origination of actions and produce blindness and lack of knowledge. Without recognizing impermanence, stress, and not-self, one remains subject to future suffering. Therefore, for a monk who lives without devoting himself to the development of these qualities, his mind will not be released from effluents through lack of clinging.

### Resolution

The development of these seven perceptions fundamentally alters one's relationship with the world, leading to profound liberation and the ending of suffering:

*   **Perception of Inconstancy (Anicca):** By developing the perception that **"All fabrications are inconstant"** and stressful, one directly awakens to and makes plain this universal truth. This leads the mind to shrink away from gains, offerings, and fame, bending, pulling back, and becoming disinterested in worldly attachments. The realization of inconstancy is highly fruitful, even for a brief moment.
*   **Perception of Not-Self (Anatta):** Cultivating the perception that **"All phenomena are not-self"** is crucial. This directly addresses the tendency for "I-making" and "my-making," leading to their cessation. This insight allows one to abandon desire-passion for whatever is not-self, including form, feeling, perception, fabrications, and consciousness.
*   **Perception of Unattractiveness (Asubha):** By committing to the "theme of the unattractive," one develops a stance of loathsomeness regarding the deceptive appearance of beauty. This helps to subdue passion and delight connected to the physical body and sensory forms.
*   **Perception of Drawbacks (Ādīnava):** This involves discerning the inherent stress and despair in all conditioned phenomena, particularly sensual pleasures. Recognizing these drawbacks leads to disenchantment with forms, feelings, perceptions, fabrications, and consciousness. This disenchantment prompts a turn away from worldly allure.
*   **Perception of Abandoning (Pahāna):** As one focuses on the inconstancy, stress, and not-self nature of phenomena, the mind is directed toward **abandoning desire-passion** for all that is inconstant, stressful, and not-self. This abandoning of desire and passion for the five clinging-aggregates is declared as the cessation of stress.
*   **Perception of Dispassion (Virāga):** Dispassion is considered supreme, involving the **"subduing of intoxication, the elimination of thirst, the uprooting of attachment, the breaking of the round, the destruction of craving, cessation, the realization of Unbinding"**. It is a quality that is part of the path to purify vision. As one develops dispassion, the mind becomes increasingly unfettered and released.
*   **Perception of Cessation (Nirodha):** This perception recognizes that the cessation of delight leads to the cessation of suffering and stress. By understanding and realizing cessation, one can touch **"liberation with the heart, the state of peace unsurpassed"**. The ultimate goal is the ending of effluents, where "birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done," signifying nothing further to be achieved in this world.

Collectively, these perceptions are part of the Dhamma taught by the Tathāgata that leads to **"disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, calming, direct knowledge, self-awakening, unbinding"**. They represent the path by which defiling mental qualities are abandoned, and bright mental qualities grow, leading to the culmination of discernment and a joyful, pleasant abiding. Ultimately, developing these perceptions enables one to become **"totally unbound"** and **"effluent-free,"** having rooted out craving and put an end to suffering and stress. This comprehensive path is for the sake of the **"right ending of suffering & stress"**.
