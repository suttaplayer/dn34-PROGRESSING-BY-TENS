Here is a detailed analysis of the statement "Which seven dhammas should be comprehended? Seven stations of consciousness" from the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34):

***

**Situation:**

In the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Venerable Sāriputta presents a comprehensive framework of Dhamma teachings, categorized by numerical groupings for clarity and systematic study. Under the "SEVENS" section, he identifies "Seven stations of consciousness" as dhammas that *should be comprehended*. These stations are presented as specific states of being or existence that involve different combinations of body and perception. The instruction is for monks to comprehend these seven stations as part of their practice to attain unbinding and end suffering and stress.

**Complication:**

The brief mention of the "seven stations of consciousness" requires a deeper understanding beyond mere listing to truly "comprehend" them. The very nature of consciousness itself is dependently co-arisen and not a permanent, independently existing entity. Failing to comprehend these stations could lead to attachment and renewed becoming. Moreover, the Blessed One emphasizes that his teachings, including those on consciousness, are to be realized by the observant for themselves, not merely accepted as hearsay. Therefore, understanding the characteristics and implications of each station is crucial for genuine spiritual progress.

**Resolution:**

To comprehend the seven stations of consciousness involves understanding their nature as compounded phenomena, their dependent origination, and the path to their cessation or transcendence. These stations illustrate the various planes of existence where consciousness can take root based on different conditions of body and perception. The Blessed One teaches these not as fixed realities to be clung to, but as phenomena to be thoroughly understood and ultimately abandoned for liberation.

The seven stations of consciousness are:

1.  **Beings with multiplicity of body and multiplicity of perception**: This refers to states like human beings, some devas, and some beings in the lower realms, where both physical form and mental perceptions are diverse and varied.
2.  **Beings with multiplicity of body and singularity of perception**: An example given is the devas of Brahmā's retinue generated by the first jhāna (meditative absorption). This implies a physical form but a unified state of mind or perception.
3.  **Beings with singularity of body and multiplicity of perception**: The Radiant Devas are an example of this station, indicating a subtle, singular form but diverse perceptions.
4.  **Beings with singularity of body and singularity of perception**: This category includes the Abhassara Devas (Refulgent Devas), suggesting both a singular body and a unified perception.
5.  **Beings who, with the complete transcending of physical form, the disappearance of perceptions of resistance, and not attending to perceptions of multiplicity, perceive 'Infinite space,' and arrive at the dimension of the infinitude of space**. This is a formless realm, a higher state of concentration (the fourth emancipation). Consciousness here is bound to the concept of infinite space.
6.  **Beings who, with the complete transcending of the dimension of the infinitude of space, perceive 'Infinite consciousness,' and arrive at the dimension of the infinitude of consciousness**. In this station, consciousness is not merely present but is the primary object of focus and is experienced as boundless (the fifth emancipation).
7.  **Beings who, with the complete transcending of the dimension of the infinitude of consciousness, perceive 'There is nothing,' and arrive at the dimension of nothingness**. This signifies a further refinement of consciousness, where the perception of infinite consciousness is transcended, leading to a state of nothingness (the sixth emancipation).

The path to comprehending these stations and ultimately achieving unbinding involves recognizing their impermanence, stress, and not-self nature. A concentrated monk discerns the inconstancy of consciousness, which is crucial for the ending of effluents. The Blessed One explains that "consciousness exists when name-&-form exists. From name-&-form as a requisite condition comes consciousness". This highlights the dependent co-arising of consciousness, meaning it does not wander on from birth to birth as an unchanging entity, but rather arises based on requisite conditions.

The ultimate aim of the holy life is the abandoning of passion, aversion, and delusion. This comprehension leads to disenchantment, dispassion, and cessation, ultimately resulting in release from clinging or sustenance. The Dhamma taught by the Blessed One leads to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, calming, direct knowledge, self-awakening, and unbinding. By understanding these various "stations" as states to be comprehended and transcended, a practitioner moves towards the effluent-free awareness-release and discernment-release, which represents the complete ending of suffering and stress.
