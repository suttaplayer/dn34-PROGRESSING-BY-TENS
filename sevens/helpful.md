In the DASUTTARA SUTTA, Venerable Sāriputta presents a framework of Dhammas, which includes the seven noble treasures as qualities that are "very helpful". An analysis of the detailed meaning of this statement can be provided using the Situation, Complication, and Resolution (SCR) format.

**Situation**
The inherent human condition is one of suffering, characterized by being "beset by birth, aging, & death, by sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, & despairs, beset by stress, overcome with stress". Beings are described as "hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving", and some are "lost... in that they don't get to hear this Dhamma sequence". This points to a fundamental state of entanglement in the world and a lack of clear direction towards lasting peace.

**Complication**
Without the cultivation of specific virtuous qualities, individuals remain trapped in this cycle of suffering. For instance, being "unvirtuous," "envious," or "stingy" leads to being "placed in hell". A person who holds "wrong view" and other unskillful qualities is "incapable of obtaining results" even if they follow a holy life. Furthermore, those who fail to recognize and abandon wrong views or intentions risk long-term harm and suffering. Such individuals are unable to effectively abandon unskillful qualities or to truly understand the path to release, perpetuating their spiritual "poverty".

**Resolution**
The resolution lies in cultivating the **seven noble treasures**, which are described as "very helpful" and "lead to a monk's non-decline". Possessing these treasures means one is "not poor" and has "not lived in vain". These treasures provide the necessary foundation for progress and ultimately, liberation:

*   **The Treasure of Conviction (Saddhā)** is defined as being "convinced of the Tathāgata's awakening: 'Indeed, the Blessed One is worthy & rightly self-awakened, consummate in clear-knowing & conduct, well-gone, an expert with regard to the cosmos, unexcelled trainer of people fit to be tamed, teacher of devas & human beings, awakened, blessed'". This conviction sets the mind "straight," leading to "a sense of the goal, gains a sense of the Dhamma, gains joy connected with the Dhamma," which in turn leads to rapture, calm, ease, and concentration. It signifies that the "doors to the deathless" are "open" to those who demonstrate it.

*   **The Treasure of Virtue (Sīla)** involves living "restrained in accordance with the Pāṭimokkha, consummate in his behavior & sphere of activity," and training oneself "seeing danger in the slightest faults". This includes abstaining from actions like taking life, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter. Virtue is fundamental, as "concentration nurtured with virtue is of great fruit, great reward". It also illustrates that ethical conduct is more beneficial than ritual, as "if you don't tell a lie, don't harm living beings, don't take what's not given, if you have conviction & are free from stinginess, what would you gain from going to the Gayā? Even a well would be Gayā for you".

*   **The Treasure of a Sense of Shame (Hiri)** indicates modesty and a lack of self-aggrandizement. A person with this quality "does not want it to be known that 'He is modest'". The Dhamma itself is "for one who is modest, not for one who is self-aggrandizing".

*   **The Treasure of a Sense of Compunction (Ottappa)** refers to a wholesome dread of wrongdoing and its consequences. It manifests as being "steadfast, solid in his effort, not shirking his duties with regard to skillful qualities", implying diligence in avoiding unskillful actions.

*   **The Treasure of Listening (Suta)** to the Dhamma allows one to "hear what one has not heard before," "clarify what one has heard before," "get rid of doubt," have their "views made straight," and their "mind grows serene". Listening to the True Dhamma is "a factor for stream entry", because the Dhamma is "well taught by the Blessed One, to be seen here & now, timeless, inviting verification, pertinent, to be experienced by the observant for themselves". When practiced with "appropriate attention," it leads to joy, rapture, calm, pleasure, concentration, and ultimately disenchantment, dispassion, and release.

*   **The Treasure of Generosity (Cāga)** means living with an "awareness cleansed of the stain of stinginess, freely generous, openhanded, delighting in being magnanimous, responsive to requests, delighting in the distribution of alms". This practice is seen as salvaging one's "wealth," as "what's given is well salvaged", and is described as "supreme & unsurpassed".

*   **The Treasure of Discernment (Paññā)** is described as being "endowed with discernment of arising & passing away—noble, penetrating, leading to the right ending of stress". This includes a deep understanding of the "four noble truths: stress, the origination of stress, the cessation of stress, the path of practice leading to the cessation of stress". Discernment also enables one to "discern what is unskillful, discerns the root of what is unskillful, discerns what is skillful, and discerns the root of what is skillful". When "nurtured with concentration," discernment "is of great fruit, great reward" and leads to the mind being "rightly released from the effluents".
