The statement, "Which three dhammas are on the side of decline? Three roots of what is unskillful: greed as a root of what is unskillful, aversion as a root of what is unskillful, delusion as a root of what is unskillful," outlines fundamental defilements that impede spiritual progress. This framework highlights these three as core factors leading to negative outcomes and the deterioration of wholesome qualities.

***

**Situation: The Three Roots of Unskillful Qualities**

The sources identify **greed, aversion, and delusion** as the **three roots of what is unskillful**. These are not merely undesirable traits, but foundational causes that give rise to suffering and impede spiritual development. They are considered **inside stains, inside enemies, inside foes, inside murderers, inside adversaries**, fundamentally harming the very person from whom they arise, much like the fruiting of a bamboo plant destroys the stalk that bore it. Furthermore, ignorance (a form of delusion) is described as preceding the arrival of other unskillful qualities, with a lack of shame and compunction following in its wake.

**Complication: The Manifestation and Consequences of Decline**

The presence of greed, aversion, and delusion leads to a range of unskillful actions and states, hindering both individual and collective spiritual welfare:

*   **Unskillful Actions and Thinking**: A person overcome by these roots fabricates **unskillful qualities by body, speech, or intellect**, potentially inflicting suffering on others through beating, imprisonment, or false accusations. This encompasses actions such as taking life, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle chatter, covetousness, and ill will, all stemming from these roots. Unskillful thinking, specifically thoughts imbued with sensuality (greed), ill-will (aversion), or harmfulness (delusion), **produces blindness, lack of vision, lack of knowledge, leads to the cessation of discernment, sides with vexation, and is not conducive to Unbinding**.
*   **Obstacles to Progress**: These three roots are key components of the **five hindrances** (sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty). These hindrances are described as **obstacles, hindrances that overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment**. Without abandoning these, an individual finds it **impossible to understand what is for their own benefit, for the benefit of others, or to realize a superior human state**.
*   **Inaction and Bewilderment**: Certain wrong views, particularly those rooted in delusion, can lead to **inaction**. For instance, believing that everything is caused by past actions or is without cause and condition eliminates the desire or effort to discern what should or shouldn't be done, leaving one **bewildered and unprotected**.
*   **Deterioration of the Dhamma**: The persistence of these unskillful qualities, especially among practitioners, can lead to the **confusion and disappearance of the True Dhamma**. This decline is not caused by external factors like earth or water, but by "**worthless people** who arise right within the Saṅgha". Such individuals may disregard the deep, transcendent discourses of the Tathāgata, preferring "literary works" or being resistant to correction.
*   **Personal Decline**: A monk in training who indulges in **excessive activity, chatter, or sleep**, or who is **restless**, is deemed **incapable of touching superlative self-awakening**. This illustrates how these unskillful tendencies contribute to a personal falling away from the path.
*   **The Cycle of Ignorance**: Ignorance (delusion) itself is sustained by a chain of conditions, starting with the **five hindrances**, which are in turn "fed" by the three forms of misconduct (bodily, verbal, mental), lack of sense restraint, lack of mindfulness and alertness, inappropriate attention, lack of conviction, and failing to hear the true Dhamma. This interconnectedness shows how these unskillful roots perpetuate suffering.

**Resolution: The Path to Abandonment and Unbinding**

The resolution to this state of decline lies in the **abandonment of greed, aversion, and delusion**. This is achieved by cultivating their opposites: **lack of greed, lack of aversion, and lack of delusion**, which are identified as the **roots of what is skillful**.

*   **Inner Peace and Release**: When these evil, unskillful qualities are **abandoned and their root destroyed**, likened to a palmyra stump deprived of conditions for future growth, one can **dwell in ease right in the here & now—feeling unthreatened, placid, unfeverish—and is unbound right in the here & now**. This leads to **awareness-release and discernment-release** as passion and ignorance fade. A monk who has **no "I-making" or "mine-making" conceit-obsession** is described as having **cut through craving, ripped off the fetter, and put an end to suffering & stress**.
*   **The Well-Taught Dhamma**: The Dhamma taught by the Blessed One is for the **abandoning of passion, aversion, and delusion**, and those who follow it **practice well** and are considered "Well-Gone" when these defilements are eradicated. The **Noble Eightfold Path** (right view, right resolve, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration) is the precise method leading to the **cessation of action**, and ultimately to **stilling, direct knowledge, self-awakening, and unbinding**.
*   **Qualities of a Wise Person**: The Dhamma is for those who embody qualities such as **modesty, contentment, reclusiveness, aroused persistence, established mindfulness, concentration, and discernment**, and who **enjoy non-objectification**. These qualities foster dispassion, shedding of unskillful traits, and liberation from entanglement.
*   **Preserving the True Dhamma**: To ensure the **stability and non-disappearance of the True Dhamma**, monks are encouraged to learn suttas thoroughly, be receptive to correction, and for learned monks to diligently teach the Dhamma. Maintaining **respect for the Teacher, Dhamma, Saṅgha, Training, and concentration** is also crucial in preventing the Dhamma's decline.
*   **Conducive to Unbinding**: The Dhamma proclaimed by the Tathāgata, having been directly known and realized by him, is inherently **conducive to the goal, conducive to the Dhamma, and basic to the holy life**, leading directly to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, stilling, direct knowledge, self-awakening, and unbinding.
