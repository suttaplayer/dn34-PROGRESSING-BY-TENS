Venerable Sāriputta's statement that "Three feelings: a feeling of pleasure, a feeling of pain, a feeling of neither pleasure nor pain" should be comprehended, briefly encapsulates a fundamental aspect of the Dhamma. This statement can be analyzed using the Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) framework to elaborate on its detailed meaning.

### Situation

The fundamental **situation** is the universal human experience of feelings. The sources describe three main types of feelings: **a feeling of pleasure, a feeling of pain, and a feeling of neither pleasure nor pain**. These feelings are interconnected with our experience of the world. The origination of all feelings comes from **contact**. For instance, when seeing a form via the eye, consciousness arises, and the meeting of these three (eye, form, eye-consciousness) is contact; with contact as a requisite condition, feeling arises. The Blessed One has described feelings in various expositions, ranging from two, three, five, six, eighteen, to one hundred and eight feelings, depending on the context of the teaching.

### Complication

The **complication** arises from the human tendency to react to these feelings without full comprehension, leading to suffering and attachment. An **uninstructed run-of-the-mill person**, when touched with a feeling of pain, "sorrows, grieves, & laments, beats his breast, becomes distraught," thereby experiencing **two pains: physical & mental**, similar to being shot by two arrows. This suffering stems from not discerning the nature of feelings. Specifically, if one does not discern "the origination, passing away, allure, drawback, or escape" from a feeling, "then one's ignorance-obsession gets obsessed".

Furthermore, if a monk "relishes, welcomes, and remains fastened to" forms cognizable via the eye that are linked to sensual desire, "there arises delight. From the origination of delight, I tell you, comes the origination of suffering & stress". This highlights how clinging to feelings, especially pleasant ones, perpetuates stress. The Tathāgata discerns that various theoretical standpoints, when "seized, thus grasped at, lead to such & such a destination, to such & such a state in the world beyond". This implies that an uncomprehended engagement with feelings, often embedded in such views, is a pathway to continued suffering.

### Resolution

The **resolution** involves comprehending these three feelings to achieve liberation from suffering. This comprehension is achieved through **right discernment**. A well-instructed disciple of the noble ones actively "discerns feelings, how feelings come into play, where they cease, & the path to their ending". This process begins by cultivating **disenchantment** with feelings.

The key to comprehension lies in understanding the inherent characteristics of feelings:
*   A **feeling of pleasure should be seen as stressful**.
*   A **feeling of pain should be seen as an arrow**.
*   A **feeling of neither pleasure nor pain should be seen as inconstant**.
*   Broadly, **whatever is inconstant is stressful**, and "whatever is stressful is not-self". Therefore, for any feeling that is "inconstant, stressful, subject to change," it is not fitting to regard it as "mine, my self, or what I am". Accordingly, one "should abandon desire-passion" for whatever is stressful and whatever is not-self.

The path to the **cessation of feeling** is the **noble eightfold path**. By cultivating this path and being "mindful, alert" to how feelings "arise, persist, and subside", one achieves **dispassion**. When feelings are comprehended, one is "totally released from sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, & despairs," and thus "totally released... from suffering & stress". This comprehension also leads to the realization of the **Deathless**.

The ultimate aim of comprehending feelings is to attain the **cessation of perception and feeling**. This state, though involving nothing felt, is described as **pleasant**, because "wherever pleasure is found, in whatever terms, the Blessed One describes it as pleasure". Such complete release is experienced "right within" when there is "lack of clinging or sustenance". This profound liberation means the monk "is called a monk who is noble, who has seen rightly, who has cut off craving, destroyed the fetters, and who—from the right breaking-through of conceit—has put an end to suffering & stress".
