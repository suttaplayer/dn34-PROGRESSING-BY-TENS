Here is an analysis of the statement regarding the three concentrations to be developed, presented in a Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) format:

**Situation: The Cultivation of Three Concentrations**

The sources indicate that among the Dhammas to be developed are three specific concentrations: **"concentration with directed thought & evaluation, concentration without directed thought & with a modicum of evaluation, concentration without directed thought & evaluation"**. These forms of concentration are crucial for spiritual progress. More specifically, a monk "should develop this concentration with directed thought & evaluation, you should develop it with no directed thought & a modicum of evaluation, you should develop it with no directed thought & no evaluation, you should develop it accompanied by rapture… not accompanied by rapture… endowed with a sense of enjoyment; you should develop it endowed with equanimity".

These concentrations correspond to the progressive stages of meditative absorption (jhāna):

*   The **first concentration**, "concentration with directed thought & evaluation", is described as entering and remaining in "rapture & pleasure born of seclusion, accompanied by directed thought & evaluation". This state is achieved when one is "quite secluded from sensuality, secluded from unskillful qualities".
*   The **second concentration**, "concentration without directed thought & with a modicum of evaluation", involves "the stilling of directed thoughts & evaluations" and is characterized by "rapture & pleasure born of concentration, unification of awareness free from directed thought & evaluation—internal assurance".
*   The **third concentration**, "concentration without directed thought & no evaluation", is attained with "the fading of rapture" where the practitioner "remains equanimous, mindful, & alert, and senses pleasure with the body". This stage is renowned by noble ones as "Equanimous & mindful, he has a pleasant abiding".

**Complication: Obstacles to Developing Concentration**

Despite the clear path outlined, several factors can hinder the development of these concentrations. Fundamentally, **"Defiled by passion, the mind is not released. Defiled by ignorance, discernment does not develop"**. These primary defilements—greed, aversion, and delusion—are identified as "inside stains, inside enemies, inside foes, inside murderers, inside adversaries" and as "cause[s] for the origination of actions". They "produce blindness, produce lack of vision, produce lack of knowledge, lead to the cessation of discernment".

Furthermore, intellectual and spiritual complacency can be significant barriers. Assemblies "trained in bombast and not in cross-questioning" may neglect the profound discourses of the Tathāgata, which are "deep, deep in their meaning, transcendent, connected with emptiness". Instead, they focus on "literary works—the works of poets, artful in sound, artful in expression, the work of outsiders, words of disciples". Such individuals "don't listen, don't lend ear, don't set their hearts on knowing them, don't regard them as worth grasping or mastering". A person is also deemed a "fool" if "He formulates a question inappropriately. He answers a question inappropriately. When another answers a question appropriately… he doesn't approve of it".

Beyond these, a lack of right view can lead to inaction and hinder progress: **"When one falls back on lack of cause and lack of condition as being essential, monks, there is no desire, no effort (at the thought), 'This should be done. This shouldn't be done.'"**. Moreover, an unbalanced approach to meditative themes can impede proper concentration. If a monk "were to attend solely to the theme of concentration, it's possible that his mind would tend to laziness. If he were to attend solely to the theme of uplifted energy, it's possible that his mind would tend to restlessness. If he were to attend solely to the theme of equanimity, it's possible that his mind would not be rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents". Ultimately, the "five hindrances—imperfections of awareness that weaken discernment" must be overcome to progress.

**Resolution: The Fruits of Developed Concentration**

Developing these concentrations is integral to the spiritual path, leading to profound liberation and the cessation of suffering. The **"Dhamma is well taught by the Blessed One, to be seen here & now, timeless, inviting verification, pertinent, to be experienced by the observant for themselves"**. This path, **"the direct path for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow & lamentation, for the disappearance of pain & distress, for the attainment of the right method, & for the realization of unbinding—in other words, the four establishings of mindfulness"**, ultimately leads to the end of suffering. "This path is pursued by those great in purpose, great seers. Those who follow it… will put an end to suffering & stress". Indeed, "Of paths, the eightfold is best. Of truths, the four sayings. Of qualities, dispassion".

A crucial prerequisite is the cultivation of virtue. When "bodily misconduct is abandoned, good bodily conduct is developed; verbal misconduct is abandoned, good verbal conduct is developed; mental misconduct is abandoned, good mental conduct is developed; wrong view is abandoned, right view is developed, then he has no fear of death as it relates to the next life". This ethical foundation supports mental development.

By attending "periodically to the theme of concentration; he should attend periodically to the theme of uplifted energy; he should attend periodically to the theme of equanimity", the mind becomes **"pliant, malleable, luminous, & not brittle. It is rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents"**. This leads to the fundamental goal of Dhamma practice: **"From the fading of passion is there awareness-release. From the fading of ignorance is there discernment-release"**.

Ultimately, through this development, the practitioner's "mind nurtured with discernment is rightly released from the effluents, i e, the effluent of sensuality, the effluent of becoming, the effluent of ignorance". This "ending of effluents is for one who knows & sees", leading to the state where the monk "has cut through craving, has ripped off the fetter, and—from rightly breaking through conceit—has put an end to suffering & stress". The goal is to be **"rightly released from the effluents"**, attaining "awareness-release & discernment-release".
