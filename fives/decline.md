In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Dīgha Nikāya 34), Venerable Sāriputta outlines various categories of Dhammas that progress by tens, covering aspects from those that are helpful to those that should be realized. Within this comprehensive framework, he identifies five "mental blockages" that lead to a monk's decline.

**Situation:**
The specific excerpt from the DASUTTARA SUTTA details certain mental states that hinder a monk's spiritual progress. These are described as "mental blockages" that prevent the mind from tending towards ardency, commitment, perseverance, or exertion.

**Complication:**
The five mental blockages, as stated in the source, are:

*   **Doubt, uncertainty, and indecisiveness about the Teacher:** A monk who is doubtful, uncertain, or indecisive about the Teacher and lacks confidence in them will find their mind unable to lean towards ardency, commitment, perseverance, or exertion.
*   **Doubt, uncertainty, and indecisiveness about the Dhamma:** Similarly, doubt, uncertainty, or indecisiveness regarding the Dhamma prevents the mind from tending towards ardency, commitment, perseverance, or exertion. The sources also describe uncertainty as an "obstacle" and "hindrance that overwhelms awareness and weakens discernment".
*   **Doubt, uncertainty, and indecisiveness about the Saṅgha:** A lack of confidence or clarity concerning the community of monks (Saṅgha) also acts as a mental blockage, hindering ardency, commitment, perseverance, and exertion.
*   **Doubt, uncertainty, and indecisiveness about the training:** When a monk is uncertain about the training or the path of practice, their mind is similarly disinclined towards diligent effort.
*   **Anger and unhappiness with fellow practitioners:** If a monk becomes angered and unhappy with their companions in the holy life, leading to a rigid and hostile mind, this state also prevents the mind from engaging in ardency, commitment, perseverance, or exertion. Such states of "ill will" are considered "defilements of the mind".

These blockages signify a decline in skillful qualities, as they prevent the necessary mental states for spiritual development. The presence of such negative qualities means that the mind is "neither pliant nor malleable nor luminous, but brittle & not rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents". When the mind is "conquered by greed, conquered by aversion, delusion, anger, hostility, hypocrisy, spite, selfishness, evil envy, or evil longing," it indicates a lack of discernment.

**Resolution:**
Overcoming these mental blockages involves cultivating their direct opposites and engaging in practices that strengthen the mind and clarify understanding.

*   **Cultivating Confidence:** The primary antidote to doubt and uncertainty is to cultivate **conviction** and **confidence** in the Teacher, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha. When a disciple recollects the Tathāgata, the Dhamma, or the Saṅgha, their mind is not overcome with passion, aversion, or delusion, but "heads straight". This straightness of mind leads to a "sense of the goal," "sense of the Dhamma," and "joy connected with the Dhamma," which in turn fosters rapture, calm, ease, and concentration. Having vision "made straight" and conviction in the Saṅgha indicates a well-founded practice.
*   **Abandoning Ill Will and Cultivating Goodwill:** To counteract anger and unhappiness towards others, a monk should develop **goodwill**. This involves pervading the world with "an awareness imbued with goodwill equal to space—abundant, enlarged, immeasurable, free from hostility, free from ill will". Reflecting on the drawbacks of unskillful thoughts, such as anger, can lead to their abandonment. The source explicitly lists "nine ways of subduing hatred" by reflecting that "He has done me harm. But what should I expect?".
*   **Developing Skillful Qualities and Discernment:** The general approach to overcoming mental blockages is to keep one's **persistence aroused for abandoning unskillful qualities and taking on skillful qualities**. The Dhamma itself is for those who are **modest, content, reclusive, persistent, mindful, concentrated, and discerning**. Such qualities lead to dispassion, being unfettered, shedding, modesty, contentment, reclusiveness, aroused persistence, and being unburdensome.
*   **Engaging with the Dhamma:** Actively engaging with the Dhamma is crucial. Listening to the Dhamma helps clarify what one has heard, gets rid of doubt, straightens one's views, and makes the mind serene. This can be achieved through various means such as:
    *   Being taught the Dhamma by a teacher or fellow practitioner.
    *   Teaching the Dhamma to others in detail.
    *   Reciting the Dhamma in detail.
    *   Directing thoughts to the Dhamma, evaluating it, and mentally examining it.
    *   Developing concentration based on a well-grasped theme, attended to and penetrated with discernment.
    These activities lead to joy, rapture, calm, pleasure, and a concentrated mind.
*   **Ardent and Resolute Practice:** Ultimately, a monk must dwell "heedful, ardent, & resolute" to release the unreleased mind, end effluents, and achieve safety from bonds. This practice, when developed and pursued, can lead to "gnosis right in the here-&-now, or—if there be any remnant of clinging-sustenance—non-return". The stability, non-confusion, and non-disappearance of the True Dhamma are directly supported by monks learning suttas well, being easy to correct, attentively teaching, and making effort for attainment. Such monks are described as having conviction, a sense of shame, compunction, aroused persistence, and discernment, enabling them to attain arahantship.
