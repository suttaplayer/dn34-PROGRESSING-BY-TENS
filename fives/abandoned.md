In the DASUTTARA SUTTA (Digha Nikaya 34), Venerable Sāriputta presents a framework of Dhammas, which includes **five hindrances**: sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty. These hindrances are critical obstacles in the path of spiritual development.

Here is an analysis of this statement using the Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR) framework:

**Situation: The Nature and Impact of the Five Hindrances**

The five hindrances are described as **obstacles and hindrances that overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment**. When a monk's mind is defiled by these hindrances, it becomes **unpliant, unmalleable, not luminous, and brittle**, making it difficult to be rightly concentrated for the ending of effluents. If these hindrances are not abandoned, a person is **without strength and weak in discernment**, rendering them incapable of understanding what benefits themselves or others, or of realizing a higher state of knowledge and vision. The sources explicitly state that these hindrances are "inside stains, inside enemies, inside foes, inside murderers, inside adversaries". Furthermore, the five hindrances are identified as the "food" for ignorance.

**Complication: How Hindrances Arise and Manifest**

The root causes of these hindrances are **greed, aversion (ill will), and delusion**, which are considered roots of what is unskillful. Thinking imbued with sensuality, ill-will, or harmfulness (aversion) is said to "produce blindness, produce lack of vision, produce lack of knowledge, lead to the cessation of discernment, side with vexation, and are not conducive to Unbinding". Specifically, sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty are the five defilements. Sensual desire and ill will, whether internal or external, function as hindrances.

Sensual pleasures, which fuel sensual desire, are characterized as providing **"little satisfaction, much stress, much despair, & greater drawbacks"**. They are vividly compared to various undesirable things such as "a chain of bones, a lump of flesh, a grass torch, a pit of glowing embers, a dream, borrowed goods, the fruits of a tree, a butcher's ax and chopping block, swords and spears, [or] a snake's head". Craving, a key component related to sensual desire, is identified as a **"fetter"** that causes beings to endlessly wander and transmigrate. This craving can take birth for mundane things like cloth, alms food, lodging, or even for the sake of becoming or not becoming. It is impossible for a person to indulge in sensual pleasures without sensual passion, perception, or thinking.

**Resolution: Overcoming the Hindrances for Release**

The path to overcoming these hindrances and achieving release involves specific practices and attitudes:

*   To abandon these five hindrances, one should primarily develop the **four establishings of mindfulness**. These are focused on the body, feelings, mind, and mental qualities, with ardency, alertness, and mindfulness to subdue greed and distress. Developing these for even seven days can lead to "gnosis right here & now, or—if there be any remnant of clinging-sustenance—non-return".
*   When the mind is **freed from these five defilements**, it becomes **pliant, malleable, and luminous**, rightly concentrated for the ending of the effluents. This allows one to directly know and realize qualities when there is an opening.
*   Abandoning these hindrances is crucial for attaining higher meditative states (jhanas) and the fruits of the contemplative life, such as stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arahantship.
*   Developing **immeasurable concentration** (based on goodwill, compassion, empathetic joy, or equanimity) allows for five realizations, including the ability to mindfully enter and emerge from concentration.
*   A key practice involves training oneself so that "In reference to the seen, there will be only the seen. In reference to the heard, only the heard. In reference to the sensed, only the sensed. In reference to the cognized, only the cognized". This training helps eliminate desire and passion, leading to the end of stress.
*   Recollecting the **Tathāgata, Dhamma, Saṅgha, and one's own virtues** prevents the mind from being overcome by passion, aversion, or delusion, fostering a mind that "heads straight" and gains joy, rapture, calm, and concentration.
*   A monk focused on the **foulness of the body** abandons the obsession with passion for beauty. Similarly, having mindfulness of in-and-out breathing helps eliminate "annoying external thoughts & inclinations".
*   The Dhamma is for those who are **modest, content, reclusive, persistent, mindful, concentrated, discerning, and enjoy non-objectification**, contrasting with those who are entangled by these very hindrances.
*   The **noble eightfold path**, which purifies vision, is to be developed for direct knowledge, comprehension, and the abandoning of the four floods (including sensuality and ignorance), ultimately putting an end to suffering and stress.

By developing the seven factors for awakening (mindfulness, analysis of qualities, persistence, rapture, calm, concentration, equanimity) through seclusion, dispassion, and cessation, the mind can be released from the effluents of sensuality, becoming, and ignorance, leading to the knowledge of release and the end of birth, aging, and death.
