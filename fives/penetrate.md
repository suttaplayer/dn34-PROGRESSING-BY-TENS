Here is an analysis of the detailed meaning of the statement "Which five dhammas are hard to penetrate? Five properties leading to escape" from the DASUTTARA SUTTA, formatted as Situation, Complication, and Resolution:

**Situation**

The Dhamma, as taught by the Blessed One, includes various categories of teachings that progress by numerical tens, aiming towards unbinding and the end of suffering. Among these teachings, Venerable Sāriputta highlights "five properties leading to escape" as particularly **hard to penetrate**. These five properties describe ways to release the mind from attachment and defilements. They are:
1.  **Escape from sensuality**: The mind, when attending to sensuality, does not delight in it, but instead, when attending to renunciation, it **leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, and released**. This leads to release from effluents, torments, and fevers that arise from sensuality.
2.  **Escape from ill will**: The mind, when attending to ill will, does not delight in it, but instead, when attending to goodwill, it **leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, and released**. This leads to release from effluents, torments, and fevers that arise from ill will.
3.  **Escape from harmfulness**: The mind, when attending to harmfulness, does not delight in it, but instead, when attending to harmlessness, it **leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, and released**. This leads to release from effluents, torments, and fevers that arise from harmfulness.
4.  **Escape from forms**: The mind, when attending to physical forms, does not delight in them, but instead, when attending to the formless, it **leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, and released**. This leads to release from effluents, torments, and fevers that arise from forms.
5.  **Escape from self-identity**: The mind, when attending to self-identity, does not delight in it, but instead, when attending to the cessation of self-identity, it **leaps up, grows confident, steadfast, and released**. This leads to release from effluents, torments, and fevers that arise from self-identity.

**Complication**

These "properties leading to escape" are deemed "hard to penetrate" because they require a profound shift in one's understanding and engagement with the world, going against deeply ingrained tendencies and common perceptions. The Dhamma itself is described as "deep, hard to see, hard to realize, tranquil, refined, beyond the scope of conjecture, subtle, to-be-experienced by the wise". For many, the path to these escapes is obscured by internal obstacles and a fundamental attachment to worldly phenomena:

*   **Hindrances and defilements**: Five major obstacles—sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty—"overwhelm awareness and weaken discernment," making it **impossible to realize a superior human state**. These hindrances prevent the mind from tending towards ardency, commitment, perseverance, or exertion, which are necessary for penetration.
*   **Worldly attachment and craving**: The general population "delights in attachment, is excited by attachment, enjoys attachment". This inclination towards attachment makes "conditionality & dependent co-arising"—the very nature of reality—**hard to see**. Craving is depicted as an "ensnarer that has flowed along, spread out, and caught hold, with which this world is smothered & enveloped like a tangled skein, a knotted ball of string, like matted rushes and reeds". Overcoming this "tangle" requires great effort.
*   **The nature of "I-making" and "mine-making"**: The escapes from forms and self-identity directly challenge the deeply held conceits of "I-making" and "mine-making" regarding one's conscious body and external themes. An uninstructed person often assumes form to be the self, or the self as possessing form, leading to sorrow and lamentation when form changes. The Dhamma avoids the extremes of eternalism and annihilationism, teaching a middle path of dependent co-arising, which is subtle and often misunderstood.
*   **Counter-intuitive nature of cessation**: Even states like the cessation of perception and feeling are described as "pleasure" by the Buddha, which can be baffling and hard to understand for those who haven't experienced it, as it is a state "where there is nothing felt".
*   **Required qualities**: Penetrating these truths requires specific mental qualities, such as being **modest, content, reclusive, persistent, mindful, concentrated, discerning, and delighting in non-objectification**, rather than their opposites. It demands "appropriate attention" and the abandonment of "inappropriate attention".

**Resolution**

Despite the inherent difficulty in penetrating these five properties, realizing them is essential for the "right ending of suffering & stress". The path to penetration leads to liberation from effluents, torments, and fevers, and ultimately to unbinding (Nibbana).

The Tathāgata, having directly awakened to these truths, declares, teaches, describes, sets forth, reveals, explains, and makes plain that "all fabrications are inconstant," "all fabrications are stressful," and "all phenomena are not-self". This knowledge forms the bedrock for achieving the "gnosis of emancipation, the breaking open of ignorance". The path to these escapes is often described as "going against the flow" of conventional existence, requiring constant effort and keen discernment. Ultimately, the ability to penetrate these properties leads to freedom from clinging and the complete cessation of suffering. This profound undertaking is for those who are willing to strive, as "Tathāgatas are simply proclaimers".

*   "Which five dhammas are hard to penetrate? But when attending to renunciation, his mind leaps up at renunciation, grows confident, steadfast, & released in renunciation. When his mind is rightly-gone, rightly developed, has rightly risen above, gained release, and become disjoined from sensuality, then whatever effluents, torments, & fevers there are that arise in dependence on sensuality, he is released from them. He does not experience that feeling. This is expounded as the escape from sensuality."
