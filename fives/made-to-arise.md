The statement "Which five dhammas should be made to arise? Right concentration with knowledges: The knowledge arises right within oneself that 'This concentration is blissful in the present & will result in bliss in the future.' 'The knowledge arises right within oneself that 'This concentration is noble & not connected with the baits of the flesh.' 'The knowledge arises right within oneself that 'This concentration is peaceful, exquisite, the acquiring of calm, the attainment of unification, not kept in place by the fabrications of forceful restraint.' 'The knowledge arises right within oneself that 'I enter into this concentration mindfully, & mindfully I emerge from it.'" from the DASUTTARA SUTTA describes a particular type of concentration and the profound realizations that accompany it. We can analyze its detailed meaning through a Situation, Complication, and Resolution (SCR) framework.

**Situation**

The human experience is characterized by **suffering and stress**. This suffering arises from various defilements such as passion, aversion, and delusion, which are considered causes for the origination of actions that lead to continued stress. Beings are beset by birth, aging, and death, along with sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, and despairs. The Dhamma, or the teaching of the Blessed One, is expounded precisely to address this condition, aiming for the **ending of this entire mass of suffering and stress**. The Dhamma is described as admirable in its beginning, middle, and end, teaching a holy life that is entirely perfect and surpassingly pure. It is meant to be seen here and now, timeless, inviting verification, pertinent, and experienced by the observant for themselves.

**Complication**

Despite the Dhamma being well-taught, individuals face various obstacles to realizing its full potential. A primary complication lies in the presence of **five hindrances**: sensual desire, ill will, sloth & drowsiness, restlessness & anxiety, and uncertainty. These hindrances defile awareness and weaken discernment, making it difficult for the mind to be rightly concentrated for the ending of effluents. Furthermore, a mind can tend to **laziness or restlessness** if it exclusively focuses on concentration or uplifted energy, respectively. People may also be "lost" if they do not get to hear the Dhamma sequence, or fail to genuinely engage with discourses that are deep, meaningful, and connected with emptiness. Instead, they might listen to literary works of poets and outsiders, without genuinely questioning or dissecting their meaning.

**Resolution**

The resolution lies in developing **"Right concentration with knowledges"** (immeasurable concentration), which, when developed and pursued mindfully and astutely, brings about **five distinct realizations**. These realizations directly address the complications and lead to the cessation of suffering:

1.  **"This concentration is blissful in the present and will result in bliss in the future."** This counters the perception of spiritual practice as solely austere, emphasizing that genuine concentration is intrinsically pleasant and leads to future well-being, such as the pleasant abidings of the jhanas.
2.  **"This concentration is noble & not connected with the baits of the flesh."** This highlights the purity of the concentration, distinguishing it from sensual pleasures and worldly attachments. It signifies a movement towards renunciation and seclusion, away from the defilements of passion.
3.  **"This concentration is not obtained by base people."** This indicates that such concentration requires a certain level of moral and spiritual cultivation. It is not easily accessible to those who are overcome by desire, aversion, and delusion, or who hold wrong views.
4.  **"This concentration is peaceful, exquisite, the acquiring of calm, the attainment of unification, not kept in place by the fabrications of forceful restraint."** This describes the profound tranquility and stability achieved. It implies a natural, unforced state of mental unification, where the mind is centered and unified, leading to calm. This state also enables discernment and the stilling of objectification.
5.  **"I enter into this concentration mindfully, and mindfully I emerge from it."** This emphasizes the **conscious control and alertness** a practitioner gains over their meditative states. It underscores the importance of established mindfulness, where one is aware of the arising, persistence, and subsiding of mental qualities.

Through the development of this concentration, coupled with the four establishings of mindfulness, one cultivates insight into the inconstancy, stress, and not-self nature of phenomena. This leads to disenchantment, dispassion, and ultimately, **release from the effluents** and the end of suffering and stress, leading to **unbinding** (Nibbana). This attainment is considered the culmination of the holy life.

The Dhamma taught by the Blessed One for this purpose is unrefuted, undefiled, blameless, and not faulted by knowledgeable contemplatives and brahmans, as it encompasses foundational truths like the six properties, six media of sensory contact, eighteen explorations for the intellect, and the four noble truths.
